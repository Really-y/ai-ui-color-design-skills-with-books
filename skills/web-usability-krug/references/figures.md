# Figure-only content, transcribed from the PDF

The text extraction of the book drops its tables and cartoon figures. The items below were read
directly from the PDF pages by the lead and verified. Use them as drop-in material: the two
"plausible excuses/reasons" tables are ready-made rebuttals for meetings, the testing table is the

## Ch. 7 — The four Home-page questions (figure, Kickstarter example)

The Home page must answer, at a glance, correctly and unambiguously, with very little effort:

1. **What is this?**
2. **What do they have here?**
3. **What can I do here?**
4. **Why should I be here — and not somewhere else?**

## Ch. 7 — The Top Four Plausible Excuses for not Spelling Out the Big Picture on the Home Page

| The excuse | Krug's rebuttal |
|---|---|
| "We don't need to. It's obvious." | When you're building a site, what you offer and why it's great is so obvious to you that it's hard to remember it isn't obvious to everybody. |
| "After people have seen the explanation once, they'll find it annoying." | Very few people avoid a site because they see the same explanation every time — unless it takes up half the page. If you know what JAMA is, "Journal of the American Medical Association" in small print next to the logo doesn't offend you. |
| "Anybody who really needs our site will know what it is." | Tempting to think people who don't "get it" aren't your real audience, but it's not true. In tests people often say "Oh, is that what it is? I'd use that all the time, but it wasn't clear what it was." |
| "That's what our advertising is for." | Even if people understood your TV/radio/web/print ads, by the time they reach the site they rarely remember what caught their interest. |

## Ch. 9 — Traditional testing vs. do-it-yourself testing (table)

| | Traditional testing | Do-it-yourself testing |
|---|---|---|
| Time per round | 1–2 days of tests, then a week to prepare a briefing or report, then some process to decide what to fix | One morning a month includes testing, debriefing and deciding what to fix; done by early afternoon |
| When do you test? | When the site is nearly complete | Continually, throughout development |
| Number of rounds | Typically one or two per project (time and expense) | One every month |
| Participants per round | Eight or more | Three |
| How are they chosen? | Recruited carefully to match the target audience | Recruited loosely, if necessary; frequent testing matters more than testing "actual" users |
| Where? | Off-site, rented facility with observation room and one-way mirror | On-site, observers in a conference room with screen-sharing software |
| Who watches? | Full days off-site, so few people observe firsthand | Half a day on-site, so more people see the tests "live" |
| Reporting | Someone spends at least a week on a briefing or a "Big Honkin' Report" (25–50 pages) | A 1–2 page email summarizing decisions made in the debriefing |
| Who identifies the problems? | The person running the tests analyzes and recommends | The whole development team and interested stakeholders meet over lunch the same day, compare notes, decide what to fix |
| Primary purpose | Identify as many problems as possible (sometimes hundreds), categorize and prioritize by severity | Identify the most serious problems and commit to fixing them before the next round |
| Out-of-pocket cost | $5,000 to $10,000 per round if you hire someone | A few hundred dollars or less per round |

## Ch. 9 — "Problems you can find with just a few test participants" (pie chart)

The pie: a large slice = problems you can find with just a few test participants; a thin
sliver = problems you have the resources to fix. Lesson: *you can find more problems in half a
day than you can fix in a month*, so three users per round is enough; focus on fixing the most
serious ones, then run another round next month.

## Ch. 9 — The Top Five Plausible Reasons for not Testing Web Sites

| The reason | Krug's rebuttal |
|---|---|
| "We don't have the time." | Schedules run on the Dilbert punchline; if testing adds to everyone's to-do list it won't happen, so make it as simple as possible. Done right it saves time: you won't (a) argue endlessly or (b) redo things at the end. |
| "We don't have the money." | Forget $5,000–$10,000. A few hundred dollars per round, even less with volunteer participants. |
| "We don't have the expertise." | The least-known fact about usability testing: it's incredibly easy to do. Some are better at it than others, but a test rarely fails to produce useful results, however poorly conducted. |
| "We don't have a usability lab." | You don't need one. A room with a desk, a computer and two chairs where you won't be interrupted, plus another room where observers can watch on a large screen. |
| "We wouldn't know how to interpret the results." | The important lessons tend to be obvious to everyone watching. The most serious problems are hard to miss. |

## Ch. 8 — "The ideal Web page as seen by someone whose job is..." (figure)

Four mock pages: **CEO** → a page that just says "PIZZAZZ!"; **Developer** → a dense
registration form with steps and fields; **Designer** → a moody, elegant visual layout;
**Business Development** → a page covered in "FREE Download / TAKE A TOUR / Start 30-Day
Trial / Register now!" buttons. Lesson: everyone projects their own professional pleasures onto
the page; none of these is the user's page.

## Ch. 12 — What developers and designers hear about accessibility (figure)

The usual arguments they are given: "It makes good business sense — people with disabilities
use the Web and have lots of money to spend." / "Everyone should have the same opportunities and
equal access to information." / "Most accessibility adaptations benefit everyone, not just people
with disabilities." / "It's a huge potential market: 65% of the population has a disability."
/ "Section 508: it's not just a good idea, it's the law."

Two of these make 22-year-old developers skeptical: the inflated "__% of the population" claim
(they'll dismiss the whole case as advocacy exaggeration) and "it benefits everyone" (closed
captioning is the only example ever cited — the "Tang" argument). The argument that actually
matters: **it's profoundly the right thing to do** — one blind person reading a newspaper on
their own is enough. And a legislative stick is coming sooner or later anyway.

What they **fear**: *more work* (one more thing in an impossible schedule, or an "initiative"
with reports and task forces) and *compromised design* ("buttered cats": good design for people
with disabilities vs. good design for everyone, forced into opposition).
