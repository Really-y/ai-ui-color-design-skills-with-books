## 1. Making people think (ch. 1)

- **"Jobs" vs. "Job-o-Rama" vs. "Employment Opportunities."** A friend says XYZ Corp is hiring; on their site the job-listings link name decides whether I find it without thinking. Internal politics or a newsletter tradition may lock a company into a cute name; the lesson is that tradeoffs should be skewed further toward "obvious" than teams assume.
- **Two flight-booking sites.** Site A: a noisy sequence of mental chatter — question marks at each step and a puzzling error at the end. Site B: takes what I type and offers sensible choices, so it's hard to go wrong — no question marks, no errors. Lesson: eliminate the mental chatter; errors are often the product of thinking the interface forced.
- **Clickable or not?** Links and buttons that aren't obviously clickable cost "a millisecond of thought" each; they add up and erode confidence in the people who built the site.

## 2. How people use the Web (ch. 2)

- **"Blah blah GINGER."** Larson's dog hears only her name; users see only words matching their task, interests, or trigger words ("Free," "Sale," their name). Lesson: the words users are looking for must be present and visible; everything else is "blah."
- **Fire commanders (Klein).** Expected to compare two options under pressure; actually took the first reasonable plan and sanity-checked it. Lesson: satisficing is the normal strategy; design for the first reasonable click being the right one.
- **The browser is "the search engine."** Many users don't know they use a browser; "a lot of people think Google is the Internet." Lesson: users muddle through successfully with wrong models; don't rely on them understanding the system.
- **"Cheap Stuff" instead of "Software."** A test participant looking for software ignores the big "Software" button and clicks "Cheap Stuff" because cheap is good. Observers go from surprise to shouting to "why bother?" Lesson: muddling works sometimes but is inefficient and error-prone; making people "get it" still matters.

## 3. Billboard design (ch. 3)

- **Stop signs and car pedals.** Conventions work because shape, word, color and location are standardized worldwide; imagine a rental car with the gas pedal on the left. Lesson: conventions let people act at a glance.
- **A page in a language you can't read.** You still know what's navigation, what's a search box, what's an article — because of conventions. Lesson: conventions carry meaning independent of content.
- **Custom scrollbars.** Designers rebuild them for looks and reveal they never considered the hundreds of hours of OS tuning they discard. Lesson: the value of a convention is easy to underestimate; replace only with something as clear or much more valuable.
- **Harlem.org.** Navigation through Art Kane's 1957 photo of 57 jazz musicians — innovative, fun and usable — plus a conventional browse by name, instrument and style in case the novelty wears off. Lesson: innovate freely, keep a conventional path.
- **"Computer Books" heading spanning all sections.** A heading placed so it appears to span the site's major sections implies they're all part of Computer Books; moving the heading where it belongs fixes the relationship. Lesson: a slightly wrong hierarchy is like "Bill put the cat on the table for a minute because it was a little wobbly."
- **Post-its over animations.** Users cover distracting animation with Post-its to read. Lesson: shouting elements get physically blocked out — or mentally (banner blindness).
- **Headings: bad vs. better.** Levels that don't look different; headings floating equidistant between paragraphs. Fix: larger/more space above for higher levels; heading tight to the text it introduces.
- **Lists: bad vs. better.** Comma series in a paragraph vs. bullets with a little space between items.

## 4. Choices and scent (ch. 4)

- **"Animal, vegetable, or mineral?"** A mindless first question in Twenty Questions, because anything not plant or animal — pianos, limericks, cheesecake — is "mineral." Lesson: a choice is mindless when its categories are exhaustive and obvious.
- **Printer manufacturer's top-level choice** (home office / small business / enterprise, etc.). "Which one was me?" — low confidence, and more thinking waiting on the target page. Compare mailboxes labeled "Stamped Mail" / "Metered Mail" with a business reply card in hand. Lesson: don't make users classify themselves into your internal segments.
- **Subscriber/member/neither article wall.** All options and conditions shown at once; the question becomes "how interested am I in this article?" NYTimes instead: one initial choice (log in / see subscription options), then only the relevant screen. Lesson: stage difficult choices; show detail only after a simple first pick.
- **"LOOK RIGHT" painted on London curbs.** Brief, timely, unavoidable guidance that has saved tourists' lives. Lesson: that is what field hints, "What's this?" links and tooltips should be.

## 5. Words (ch. 5)

- **Survey instructions: 103 → 34 words.** After: "Please help us improve the site by taking 2-3 minutes to complete this survey. NOTE: If you have comments or concerns that require a response, don't use this form. Instead, please contact Customer Service." Lesson: keep purpose, time cost and the one exception that matters; drop the rest.
- **Table-of-contents happy talk.** "This book contains many interesting chapters about ___, ___, and ___. We hope you enjoy them." — what section-front intros amount to. Lesson: delete.
- **"Welcome to..." paragraphs** and brochure prose about how great we are vs. what makes us great. Lesson: say what you do, not how wonderful you are.

## 6. Navigation (ch. 6)

- **Buying a chainsaw at Sears.** Department signs high on the walls → "Tools? Or Lawn and Garden?" → aisle signs → shelf; wrong aisle → back up; or ask a clerk. Lesson: site hierarchy and signage must support this exact process, including the ask-first (search) path.
- **No sense of scale/direction/location.** You never know whether a site has 50 or 17,000 pages; you teleport between pages; you can't remember "right rear corner near the refrigerators." Lesson: Home as North Star, Back, bookmarks, visited-link colors, and explicit "you are here" compensate for a missing sense of place.
- **The magazine with no page numbers on ad pages.** Broken convention → frustration. Lesson: navigation conventions matter precisely when you're lost.
- **Site ID framing the page.** Because the ID represents the whole site (top of the logical hierarchy), it must either dominate or frame; at the top it frames. Lesson: position encodes hierarchy.
- **Street signs in L.A. vs. Boston.** L.A.: big, hanging over the street you're on, readable from the stop line; Boston: lucky to read it in time. Lesson: page names must be big, in the right place, and present on every page.
- **"Gifts for Him" → "Gifts for Men."** Not identical, but equivalent enough that nobody thinks about it. Lesson: when exact match is impossible, match closely and make the reason obvious.
- **Bedroom > Lighting both highlighted.** Current section and subsection both marked in the nav. Lesson: mark every level, and mark it with two distinctions.
- **Hansel's pebbles vs. breadcrumbs.** Pebbles worked, crumbs got eaten — "a story about how unpleasant it is to be lost." Lesson: breadcrumb trails must be reliable and consistent.
- **Flowchart four levels deep, mockups two levels deep.** Krug flips the pages looking for "some magic happens here." Lesson: design and mock up navigation for all levels before the color debate.
- **Tabs that pop.** The active tab in a contrasting color, physically joined to the panel, reads as "in front"; without the join, they're just colored boxes.

## 7. Home page (ch. 7)

- **Beat the Clock.** "Toss five water balloons into the colander on your head... blindfolded." Lesson: the Home page always has one more job; accept compromise but never drop the big picture.
- **xkcd "University Website."** Venn diagram: what the Home page shows vs. what visitors want; "some university sites don't have the full name of the school on the Home page." Lesson: design by stakeholders buries what users came for.
- **"I see stakeholders."** Looking at Home pages, Krug feels like the boy in The Sixth Sense. Lesson: expect turf battles over waterfront property, especially above the fold.
- **Kickstarter.** A novel proposition, so almost every element of Home explains and reinforces it; "What is Kickstarter?" is the most prominent primary-nav item. Lesson: use as much space as the proposition needs.
- **Mission statement as Welcome blurb.** "XYZCorp offers world-class solutions in the burgeoning field of blah blah blah." Lesson: nobody reads it; it isn't an explanation.
- **Taglines.** MGM "More stars than there are in the heavens"; NYT "All the News That's Fit to Print." A motto like "We bring good things to life" or "You're in good hands" tells you nothing about what the thing is; NationalGrid can get away with a motto only because it's a public utility with a captive audience. Lesson: tagline = value proposition, 6–8 words, unique to you.
- **Divers bobbing to the surface.** Users who teleport in from a link then visit Home to get bearings and judge credibility. Lesson: Home must still orient even if few enter there.
- **The commons pasture.** Each herdsman gains +1 per animal and shares the overgrazing cost (< −1), so everyone adds animals until the pasture dies. Lesson: Home-page promos follow the same math; set rules before the slow slide.

## 8. Debates and testing (ch. 8–9)

- **"It came to our CEO in a dream."** A puzzling Home-page feature on an otherwise sensible site. Lesson: hype-culture edicts land on craft culture; testing moves decisions to "what works."
- **Drapes vs. windows.** Teams test to settle cascading vs. mega menus and discover nobody understands the value proposition. Lesson: test early for the big problems; aesthetics debates are rarely the problem.
- **"Launching in two weeks / two months."** Disaster check vs. settling an argument — too little, too late, wrong reasons.
- **Focus group vs. usability test.** 5–10 people talking vs. one person using; Krug made an animated video ("You say potato") because the confusion is so common. Lesson: pick the method by what you need to learn (feelings vs. whether it works).
- **15-minute workshop demo tests.** A volunteer tries a task on another attendee's site; the owner scribbles pages of notes; one team's resulting change saved an estimated $100,000. Lesson: one user, briefly, always yields something valuable.
- **Testing is friends visiting from out of town.** You see your hometown freshly and realize what's not obvious to everyone. Lesson: you can't see your own site after a few weeks.
- **"Male accountants 25–30... who recently purchased expensive shoes."** Over-specified recruiting profile. Lesson: recruit loosely, grade on a curve.
- **Currency-exchange site for money managers.** Some real money managers won't know "your" terminology; experts aren't insulted by clarity. Lesson: include non-experts even for expert products.
- **Task wording.** "You have an existing account with the username delphi21 and the password correcthorsebatterystaple. You've always used the same answers to security questions on every site, and you just read that this is a bad idea. Change your answer for this account." Lesson: supply what the participant needs but wouldn't have; give a motivating scenario. And "Find a book you want to buy, or a book you bought recently" beats "Find a cookbook for under $14."
- **Janice and eLance (sample session).** Home-page tour: likes the orange and the sun logo (ignore — the next user may hate them); reads the tagline "Where the world competes to get your job done" — "I don't know what that means"; eventually infers from the category list that it's about buying and selling services. Task (her own choice: find someone to build a deck): under "Family and Household" she must choose "RFPs" or "Fixed-Price"; she assumes Fixed-Price means a quote (wrong — RFP is the quote path). Probing afterwards reveals she noticed the five numbered step pictures but ignored them because she "wasn't ready to start the process yet," while designers assumed everyone would start there. Lessons: users don't understand your terms; they don't start where you think; save such questions for probing; let users choose task details.
- **"That functionality is all going to change soon."** The sentence by which serious problems go unfixed. Lesson: ruthless focus on the most serious problems first.
- **Feature requests that evaporate.** Asked to describe how the requested feature would work, participants conclude "I probably wouldn't use that." Lesson: participants aren't designers; a genuine great idea is recognizable instantly.
- **Kayak problems.** Rolling over and righting immediately — no harm, no foul if noticed fast, recovered unaided, not fazed.

## 9. Mobile (ch. 10)

- **CBS News on iPhone.** Tap, wait; scroll past the same photo; read two paragraphs; tap Next, wait; repeat 8 times. Krug now picks another source from Google News whenever he sees a CBS link. Lesson: a tradeoff made for page views or CMS convenience cost them a reader; most serious usability problems are bad tradeoffs.
- **Mobile First misread.** "On the move" users were assumed to need only on-the-go features (check balance, not reconcile); in reality couch users want everything. Lesson: prioritize, don't amputate.
- **AP breaking-news app.** Loads photos for every top story before showing the alert you tapped; Krug checks NYT/Google News instead. Lesson: speed first, show what was asked for before everything else.
- **SoundHound / Paper.** Delight from identifying a song and scrolling synced lyrics; from five tools with no options, each tuned to look good. Lesson: delight = impossible-seeming benefit + new tech, with usability intact.
- **Clear (to-do app).** Ten-screen tour plus a self-teaching tutorial list, yet no demo participant could create "Chicago trip" with three items — they never grasp the levels (lists / items / settings) and can't reach Help. Lesson: novel gestures need visible structure; learnability failures hide behind good reviews.
- **ASketch.** No on-screen controls; for months Krug couldn't remember how to start a new drawing (double tap? triple? edge taps? swipes?). Lesson: hidden gesture-only controls kill memorability; "life is cheap (99 cents)" so apps get abandoned.
- **Brundlefly camera.** A $30, one-hour rig (book-light clamp + webcam) attached to the phone so the participant holds it naturally while observers see screen and fingers; no face camera. Lesson: solve mobile-test logistics with a device-mounted camera, not restraints or mirroring.

## 10. Goodwill (ch. 11)

- **Airline on strike day.** Nothing about the strike on Home, in news, press releases (five days old), About Us (full of promotions), search ("strike" → last year's releases and 1950s history) or FAQs. Only one question mattered: "What's going to happen to me?" Lesson: silence on the one thing everyone needs drains the reservoir and tarnishes a brand worth hundreds of millions.
- **Airport Wi-Fi pricing.** "Wireless Access" → "Click here to connect" → three pages before a hint of cost. Lesson: hiding price to get users vested reads as a phone-sales tactic.
- **Spaces in credit-card numbers rejected.** Spaces actually make correct typing easier; the site just didn't want to write a little code. Lesson: never punish formatting.
- **"Your call is important to us."** Faux sincerity everyone sees through.
- **Visible 800 number.** Knowing they *can* call keeps people trying the site longer and more of them solve it themselves. Lesson: hiding the number costs more than it saves.
- **Amazon's tracking link.** The receipt opens the carrier's site with the tracking number submitted. Lesson: save steps.
- **HP support.** Massive effort to generate, verify, present and organize answers; Krug solves his own printer problems and keeps buying HP. Lesson: effort on support content is loyalty.
- **"Puke."** Ignore color comments in tests unless three of four participants use a word like that — it happened once; they changed the color.
- **Pop-ups at +10% revenue.** Acceptable as an informed business decision; not as an accident.

## 11. Accessibility and management (ch. 12–13)

- **Three-second accessibility test.** Increase the browser text size; "Before" and "After (no difference)" — fixed-size fonts. Lesson: the easiest signal of whether anyone tried.
- **Tang.** Arguing accessibility helps everyone with only closed captioning as evidence is like justifying the space program with Tang. Lesson: lead with "it's the right thing" (blind people reading any newspaper), and with the reverse truth — fixing general confusion is the best accessibility work.
- **Chicago taxi sign.** Print with a Plexiglas Braille overlay: both audiences get full-size, best-case treatment. Lesson: the "buttered cat" conflict usually has an elegant solution.
- **Validator as grammar checker.** Finds missing alt text, then buries you in vague warnings. Lesson: fix the obvious; don't let the list define accessibility.
- **Executives who "poke their head in."** They stay, fascinated, because it's the first time they've seen anyone use the company's site. Lesson: a live session beats any report or ROI deck.
- **Renaming a badly labeled button.** The ideal first self-funded test: one serious, quickly fixable problem; test, fix, publicize; measure support-call drop if possible.
- **Three "Start Download" buttons.** Nearly invisible instructions, decoy links, unwanted toolbar installed, search and Home page changed. Lesson: that's manipulation, "not part of your job."
- **"Job TiAssistant Managertle."** What happens when in-field labels get submitted with the input. Lesson: labels outside fields unless the five conditions hold.
