## 1. Foundations (Introduction, ch. 1)

**Usability (Krug's definition).** "A person of average (or even below average) ability and experience can figure out how to use the thing to accomplish something without it being more trouble than it's worth." Krug maps this to three attributes: learnable (figure out how to use), effective (accomplish something), efficient (not more trouble than it's worth) (Intro, ch. 10). Other attributes people cite — useful, memorable, desirable, delightful — are discussed in ch. 10; useful and desirable are marketing questions to settle before a project with interviews, focus groups, surveys.
- *Why:* keeps the bar concrete: below-average people, real tasks, effort proportional to value.
- *Pitfall:* treating usability as "what users like" (see §8).

**Expert usability review / usability testing / live debrief (Intro).** Krug's own process: try the thing doing what users would do and note where people are likely to get stuck ("expert usability review"); watch others use it ("usability testing"); meet the team live to describe issues and decide which to fix and how. He dropped the "big honking report": live presentation allows questions; Agile/Lean teams have no time for reports.

**"It's not rocket surgery."** Most of usability is common sense that isn't obvious until pointed out. There is more leverage in a few principles than in laundry lists of do's and don'ts; most answers are "it depends," so this book deliberately omits hard-and-fast rules (except the handful in §14).

**Thin-book principle (Intro, footnote).** "If something requires a large investment of time — or looks like it will — it's less likely to be used." Applies to documentation, forms, onboarding, anything.

**Krug's First Law: "Don't make me think!" (ch. 1).** The overriding principle and ultimate tie-breaker. A page should be self-evident: obvious, self-explanatory; the user "gets it" — what it is and how to use it — without expending effort.
- *Mental model:* a page that doesn't make me think produces thought balloons like "OK, there's the ___, that's a ___, there's the thing I want." A page that makes me think produces question marks. "Your job is to get rid of the question marks."
- *Self-evident vs. self-explanatory:* when something is original, groundbreaking or inherently complicated you may have to settle for self-explanatory — "it takes a little thought to get it, but only a little." Appearance (size, color, layout), well-chosen names and small amounts of carefully crafted text work together. Rule: if you can't make it self-evident, make it self-explanatory.
- *Neighbor test:* your next-door neighbor, with no interest in your subject and barely able to use the Back button, looks at the Home page and says "Oh, it's a ____."
- *Things that make us think:* names (cute/clever, marketing-induced, company-specific, unfamiliar technical); links and buttons that aren't obviously clickable; and generally: Where am I? Where should I begin? Where did they put ___? What are the most important things on this page? Why did they call it that? Is that an ad or part of the site?
- *Why it matters (not the usual reason):* competition is not the main reason — people often have no choice (intranet, bank app), tough it out blaming themselves, and competitors may be no better. Real reasons: a self-evident page is like good lighting in a store, everything seems better; puzzling over unimportant things saps energy, enthusiasm and time; and above all people spend far less time on pages than designers imagine, so pages must work at a glance.
- *Cost model:* every question mark adds cognitive workload; slight distractions add up, especially for frequent micro-decisions like what to click; carelessness erodes confidence in the site and the organization.
- *Caveat:* names sit on a continuum from "obvious to everybody" to "truly obscure" with real tradeoffs (politics, legacy names); the point is that the tradeoff should be skewed further toward obvious than teams think. "There's almost always a plausible rationale — and a good, if misguided, intention — behind every usability flaw."

## 2. How people really use the Web (ch. 2)

The gap: designers imagine users poring over each page, reading crafted text, understanding the organization, weighing options. Reality: glance, scan some text, click the first link that catches interest or vaguely resembles the target; large parts of the page are never looked at. "We're thinking 'great literature' (or at least 'product brochure'), while the user's reality is much closer to 'billboard going by at 60 miles an hour.'" It depends on page type, task, hurry — but the simple picture is closer to reality than designers' picture.

**Fact of Life #1 — We don't read pages. We scan them.** Exception: document pages (news stories, reports, product descriptions), where people read, often alternating reading and scanning. Why: we're on a mission (users act like sharks — keep moving or die); we know we don't need to read everything; we're good at scanning (learned with reading). What we see depends on what we have in mind: words that match (a) the task at hand, (b) current or ongoing personal interests, (c) hardwired trigger words — "Free," "Sale," "Sex," and our own name (the "blah blah GINGER" cartoon).

**Fact of Life #2 — We don't make optimal choices. We satisfice.** (Herbert Simon: satisfying + sufficing.) We choose the first reasonable option, not the best. Gary Klein's fire commanders didn't compare options: they took the first reasonable plan, mentally tested it for problems, and acted. Why on the Web: we're in a hurry ("optimizing is hard and takes a long time; satisficing is more efficient"); little penalty for guessing wrong (a click or two of Back — the most-used browser button); weighing options may not help on poorly designed sites; guessing is more fun. It varies with frame of mind, time pressure and confidence in the site.
- *Apply:* assume the first plausible-looking link gets clicked; make link wording and placement so the first reasonable option is the right one; keep Back cheap.

**Fact of Life #3 — We don't figure out how things work. We muddle through.** Few people read instructions; we forge ahead, inventing vaguely plausible stories about what we're doing (the pauper using the Great Seal as a nutcracker). Many users call the browser "the search engine"; "a lot of people think Google is the Internet." Not limited to beginners — tech-savvy users have surprising gaps. Why: it's not important to us (lack of caring, not intelligence); once something works, however badly, we stick with it and seldom look for a better way.
- *Why it still matters:* muddling through is inefficient and error-prone. If users "get it" instead: better chance of finding things, of understanding the full range of what the site offers, of being steered where you want them, and they feel smarter and more in control — which brings them back. "You can get away with a site that people muddle through only until someone builds one down the street that makes them feel smart."
- *Observer pattern:* first time a user clicks "Cheap Stuff" instead of "Software," observers are surprised; second time they yell at the screen; third time they think "why bother?" (The answer: because it matters.)

**Conclusion:** "If your audience is going to act like you're designing billboards, then design great billboards."

## 3. Billboard design: designing for scanning (ch. 3)

Six things to do: take advantage of conventions; create effective visual hierarchies; break pages into clearly defined areas; make it obvious what's clickable; eliminate distractions; format content to support scanning.

**Conventions.** Widely used or standardized design patterns (stop signs; gas pedal right of brake). Web conventions cover *where* things are (logo top-left in left-to-right languages; primary nav across the top or down the left), *how* things work (shopping cart, checkout form sequence), *how* things look (video-link icon, search icon, social sharing buttons), and genre conventions (commerce, colleges, blogs, restaurants, movies). They start as someone's bright idea and are imitated until they need no explanation; proof: you understand a page in a language you can't read.
- *Why designers resist:* hired to do something new; praise, awards and job offers are never for "best use of conventions."
- *Rule:* "Innovate when you know you have a better idea, but take advantage of conventions when you don't." If you replace a convention, the replacement must either (a) be so clear and self-explanatory there's no learning curve, or (b) add so much value that a small learning curve is worth it. Custom scrollbars are the classic failure — hundreds of hours of OS fine-tuning thrown away for prettiness.
- *Creativity is fine:* Harlem.org navigates through a photo of 57 jazz musicians — innovative, fun, usable — and still adds conventional category navigation (by name, instrument, style) for when the fun wears off. "Be as creative and innovative as you want... as long as you make sure it's still usable."

**Clarity trumps consistency.** Consistency within a site is worth striving for (nav always in the same place means I never look for it), but "if you can make something significantly clearer by making it slightly inconsistent, choose in favor of clarity." Beware winning arguments with "it wouldn't be consistent."

**Visual hierarchy.** Appearance must accurately portray relationships: which things are most important, which are similar, which are part of others. Three traits: (1) the more important, the more prominent — larger, bolder, distinctive color, more white space, nearer the top, or a combination; (2) logically related things are visually related — grouped under a heading, same style, in a defined area; (3) things are nested visually to show what's part of what (section name above book titles; each title spanning its description). A good hierarchy preprocesses the page; without one (everything equally important) users fall back to slow scanning for revealing words. Even a slightly flawed hierarchy (a heading spanning things that aren't part of it) is like reading "Bill put the cat on the table for a minute because it was a little wobbly" — you can work it out, but it throws you.

**Clearly defined areas.** Users should be able to play $25,000 Pyramid: point at areas and say "Things I can do on this site!", "Links to today's top stories!", "Products this company sells!", "Things they're eager to sell me!", "Navigation!" Lets users decide fast what to focus on and what to ignore; eye-tracking shows users decide in initial glances which regions are useful and rarely look at the rest (banner blindness is the extreme case).

**Make it obvious what's clickable.** Scanning for the next click relies on shape (buttons, tabs), location (menu bar), formatting (color, underlining). The hand cursor is slow to discover and doesn't exist on touch screens. Rule: one color for all text links, or shape/location that identify them; never the same color for links and non-clickable headings. (Norman's *Design of Everyday Things*: door handles.) Resurfaces as a mobile problem (ch. 10 affordances).

**Visual noise (three kinds).** *Shouting*: everything clamoring — exclamation points, many typefaces, bright colors, automated slideshows, animation, pop-ups, new ad formats; "everything can't be important" — shouting is a failure to decide what matters most. *Disorganization*: a ransacked room; sign the designer doesn't use grids to align elements. *Clutter*: too much stuff (especially Home pages); low signal-to-noise ratio. Editing stance: assume everything is noise — "presumed guilty until proven innocent" — and remove anything not making a real contribution.

**Format text to support scanning.**
- Use plenty of headings: an informal outline; "use more headings than you'd think and put more time into writing them." Two styling rules: if more than one level, make an obvious, impossible-to-miss distinction (larger, or more space above); and — more important — don't let headings float: closer to the section they introduce than to the one they follow (repeated in ch. 13 as a definitive rule).
- Keep paragraphs short: long ones are a "wall of words" (Jarrett & Redish); single-sentence paragraphs are fine; a long paragraph almost always has a place to break it in two.
- Use bulleted lists: anything that can be a list probably should be; look for series separated by commas/semicolons; add a small amount of extra space between items.
- Highlight key terms in bold at first appearance (not if already links); don't highlight too many.
- Further reading: Ginny Redish, *Letting Go of the Words*.

## 4. Mindless choices and scent (ch. 4)

**Krug's Second Law: "It doesn't matter how many times I have to click, as long as each click is a mindless, unambiguous choice."** Click-count rules (max 3/4/5) miss the point: what counts is how hard each click is — thought required and uncertainty about being right. Users tolerate many clicks if each is painless and they keep confidence they're on the right track — following the "scent of information" (Pirolli & Card, information foraging). Links that clearly, unambiguously identify their target give strong scent.
- *Rule of thumb:* "three mindless, unambiguous clicks equal one click that requires thought."
- *Exceptions:* repeated drill-down through the same path, or slow-loading pages, raise the value of fewer clicks.
- *Model of a mindless choice:* "Animal, vegetable, or mineral?" — once you accept that everything not plant/animal is mineral, it needs no thought.
- *Hard choices to avoid:* top-level audience segmentation ("which one was me?") where the user isn't confident and faces more thinking on arrival (like "Stamped Mail / Metered Mail" boxes with a business reply card); an article page presenting subscriber/member/neither options all at once, so the question shifts from "how do I answer?" to "how interested am I in this article?" NYTimes instead asks one initial choice (log in / see subscription options) and shows only relevant detail on the next screen.
- *Forms:* Caroline Jarrett, *Forms that Work*, ch. "Making Questions Easy to Answer."

**Guidance when a choice can't be made mindless.** Give as much guidance as needed, no more. Guidance works when it is *brief* (the smallest amount of information that helps), *timely* (placed exactly where/when needed), *unavoidable* (formatted so it's noticed). Forms: tips adjacent to fields, "What's this?" links, tooltips. Model: London's "LOOK RIGHT" painted on the curb.

## 5. Omit needless words (ch. 5)

**Krug's Third Law: "Get rid of half the words on each page, then get rid of half of what's left."** Removing half is realistic without losing value; "half of what's left" is deliberate exaggeration to encourage ruthlessness. Extra words signal that reading may be required, making pages seem more daunting. Benefits: lower noise, useful content more prominent, shorter pages — more visible at a glance without scrolling. Not about shortening real articles (WebMD, NYTimes).

**Happy talk must die.** Introductory text that welcomes you and says how great the site/section is. Test: a tiny voice in your head says "blah blah blah." It's self-congratulatory promotion that conveys no information — "how great we are" rather than "what makes us great." Habitats: Home pages ("Welcome to...") and especially section fronts (pages that are just link lists), like adding to a table of contents "This book contains many interesting chapters... We hope you enjoy them." Eliminate as much as possible.

**Instructions must die.** No one reads instructions until muddling through has failed; if wordy, they still won't find what they need. Objective: eliminate instructions by making things self-explanatory; when unavoidable, cut to the bare minimum (survey intro: 103 words → 34; see examples.md).

## 6. Navigation (ch. 6)

**Premise.** "People won't use your Web site if they can't find their way around it."

**The store model.** Finding a chainsaw at Sears: read department signs (big, visible across the store) → aisle signs → shelf; wrong guess → another aisle or back up a level. At the door you decide whether to look yourself or ask. On the Web: you're trying to find something; you choose ask-first (search) or browse-first; browsing = Home → sections → subsections → list → item; you leave when convinced it isn't there or too frustrated. Nielsen's "search-dominant" users look for the search box immediately; "link-dominant" users browse first and search only when out of links or frustrated; everyone else decides by mood, hurry and whether the site looks browsable.

**Why Web space is disorienting.** No sense of scale (50 pages or 17,000? hard to know when to stop looking — one reason visited links in a different color help); no sense of direction (only up/down the hierarchy); no sense of location (links teleport you; no accumulated spatial memory; you must remember the conceptual hierarchy and retrace). Hence bookmarks, the Back button, and the Home page as the North Star — a fresh start. "We're inherently lost when we're on the Web." Navigation = getting from one place to another *and* figuring out where you are. "Navigation isn't just a feature of a Web site; it is the Web site" — the building, shelves and registers *are* Sears.

**Overlooked purposes of navigation.** Beyond finding things and situating us: it tells us what's here (makes the hierarchy visible; "navigation reveals content!" — maybe its most important job); it tells us how to use the site (where to begin, what the options are — it should be all the instructions you need); it gives confidence in the people who built it (users keep a running tally: "do these guys know what they're doing?").

**Conventions for navigation.** Standard place → found quickly; standard appearance → distinguishable from everything else (street signs at corners, up high, horizontal; page numbers in margins).

**Persistent (global) navigation.** The elements on every page. Message: "The navigation is over here. Some parts will change a little depending on where you are, but it will always be here, and it will always work the same way." Same place + consistent look confirms you're still in the same site and means you learn it once. Contains: Site ID, Sections (primary navigation), Utilities, Search — and a way Home.
- *Exception — forms:* on checkout, registration, subscription, feedback and preference pages, persistent nav can be a distraction; use a minimal version: Site ID, link to Home, and utilities that help fill the form.

**Site ID.** The building name; because travel is teleportation you need it on every page, at the top, usually upper-left (LTR languages). It is the top of the logical hierarchy (site > sections > subsections > ... > this page > areas > items), so it must either be the most prominent element (only on Home, if at all) or frame everything else — hence top-of-page. It must look like a Site ID: distinctive typeface and a graphic recognizable at any size from button to billboard. Almost all users expect it to be a Home link.

**Sections (primary navigation).** Links to the top level of the hierarchy. Secondary navigation (subsections of the current section) may live in the persistent nav, in a dropdown on hover/click, or on the section front page.

**Utilities.** Important links that aren't part of the content hierarchy: help using the site (Sign in/Register, Help, Site Map, Shopping Cart) or info about the publisher (About Us, Contact Us). Slightly less prominent than Sections. Candidate list for corporate/e-commerce: About Us, Archives, Checkout, Company Info, Contact Us, Customer Service, Discussion Boards, Downloads, Directory, Forums, FAQs, Help, Home, Investor Relations, How to Shop, Jobs, My ___, News, Order Tracking, Press Releases, Privacy Policy, Register, Search, Shopping Cart, Sign in, Site Map, Store Locator, Your Account. Persistent nav holds only 4–5 (the most needed); more get lost in the crowd; the rest belong in the footer.

**Home.** A Home button/link in sight at all times is a reset button / "Get out of Jail Free" card. Site ID as Home link plus "Home" among the main sections.

**Search.** Unless the site is very small and very well organized, every page needs a search box (a box is better than a link to a search page). Users scan for the formula: a box, a button, the word "Search" or the magnifying glass. Avoid fancy wording (not Find, Quick Find, Quick Search, Keyword Search; if "Search" labels the box, the button says "Go"); avoid instructions ("Type a keyword" is "leave a message at the beep"); avoid options — spell out scope only when confusion is possible (site vs. section vs. whole Web); think very hard before scope limiters or search-by selectors (title/author, part number/name) — rarely worth making me think; offer scoping on the results page when too many hits come back.

**Lower-level navigation.** Designers deliver a 4-level flowchart but sample pages only for Home and two levels; past level 2, navigation becomes ad hoc — one of the most common problems, especially in large sites. Causes: it's hard; no time; seems unimportant ("not even secondary"); no sample content for lower pages. But users spend as much time on lower pages as at the top and it's hard to graft nav on later. Moral: sample pages showing navigation for all potential levels before arguing about colors.

**Page names (street signs).** L.A. signs are big and hang over the street you're on. Four rules: every page needs a name (highlighting it in the nav is not enough); it must be in the right place — framing the content unique to this page (not the nav or ads); it must be prominent — position, size, color, typeface say "heading for the entire page," usually the largest text on the page; it must match what I clicked — the implicit social contract: "The name of the page will match the words I clicked to get there." Discrepancies force "why are those different?" and erode trust. Compromise allowed when space forces it: match as closely as possible and make the reason obvious ("Gifts for Him" → "Gifts for Men" is fine).

**"You are here."** Highlight the current location in every nav bar, list or menu (section and subsection both marked). Most common failing: too subtle. Use more than one visual distinction (different color + bold). Designers love subtle cues; hurried users miss them: "if you're a designer and you think a visual cue is sticking out like a sore thumb, it probably means you need to make it twice as prominent."

**Breadcrumbs.** Show the path from Home to here; make it easy to move up levels. Once only on huge database sites; now common, sometimes substituting for real navigation. Done right: self-explanatory, compact, consistent way to back up a level or go Home; most useful on large, deep hierarchies. Practices: put them at the top (marginalizes them like page numbers); use `>` between levels (suggests forward motion down the levels); boldface the last item (the current page name; not a link).

**Tabs.** A physical metaphor that works (binder dividers). They're self-evident (no one asks what they do), hard to miss (people overlook horizontal nav bars; tabs are visually distinctive and clearly separate navigation from content), and slick. To work, the active tab must appear in front: different color or contrasting shade *and* physically connected to the space below it; that connection matters more than the tab shape.

**The trunk test.** Blindfolded, locked in a trunk, dumped on a random deep page: can you answer without hesitation — What site is this? (Site ID) What page am I on? (page name) What are the major sections? (Sections) What are my options at this level? (local navigation) Where am I in the scheme of things? ("You are here") How can I search? People arrive via search engines, social links and emails — abducted, not following the garden path. The blur matters: the standard is that elements pop off the page regardless of scrutiny. Procedure in trunk-test.md.

## 7. Home page (ch. 7)

**What a Home page must accommodate.** Site identity and mission (what site, what for, why here); site hierarchy (content: "what can I find," features: "what can I do," and organization — usually via persistent nav); search (prominent box); teases (content promos: newest/best/most popular; feature promos: explore sections/features); timely content (frequent updates if return visits matter; even a static site needs signs of life, e.g., a recent press release); deals (ads, cross-promotion, co-branding); shortcuts (most requested content, e.g., software updates); registration (register, sign in, "Welcome back, Steve Krug"). Abstract objectives: show me what I'm looking for; show me what I'm not looking for (expose things I might like); show me where to start; establish credibility and trust (only chance with some visitors).

**Constraints.** Everybody wants a piece — Home is the waterfront property; prominent promotion drives significantly more traffic; "I see stakeholders"; above the fold is fought over hardest because users scan down only far enough to find an interesting link. Too many cooks (even the CEO). One size fits all. Even the best Home page can't do everything on a complex site; compromise is inevitable.

**Big Bang Theory of Web Design.** The first few seconds on a new site/page are critical. Research ("You Have 50 Milliseconds to Make a Good First Impression!"): snap impressions (looks good? lots or little content? clear regions? which attract?) closely match impressions formed after spending time. But initial *understanding* is often wrong: people form wrong ideas about what the site is and force-fit everything onto that story. "If people are lost when they start out, they usually just keep getting...loster." The first casualty of Home page compromise is conveying the big picture: "They haven't made it clear enough what the site is." Everything else (impress, entice, direct, deals) has advocates; nobody has a vested interest in the main point.
- *The four questions* the Home page must answer at a glance, correctly and unambiguously (figure in the book, verified against the PDF; see `figures.md`): What is this? What can I do here? What do they have here? Why should I be here — and not somewhere else?

**"Nobody enters through the Home page anymore."** True — people arrive deep via email, blogs, social. So every page should orient (who you are, what you do, what's offered), but page space is limited; users have adopted a new behavior: teleport deep, then bob up to the Home page to get bearings (what else is here, who publishes it, is it credible). The Home page is still where that happens.

**Three places for an explicit statement of what the site is.** (1) Tagline — the space visually connected to the Site ID; read as a description of the whole site. (2) Welcome blurb — terse description in a prominent block, usually top-left or center of the content area. (3) "Learn more" — a short explanatory video; people now expect and will watch one for novel propositions. Most users guess from overall content first, but need somewhere to find out if they can't.

**Guidelines for getting the message across.** Use as much space as necessary (Kickstarter's novel proposition: almost every element explains it; "What is Kickstarter?" is the most prominent primary-nav item) — but no more than necessary (full-page messages don't get absorbed; a few important features, not all). Don't use a mission statement as a Welcome blurb ("world-class solutions in the burgeoning field of blah blah" — nobody reads it). It's one of the most important things to test: you can't trust your own judgment; show it to outsiders; "the 'main point' is the one thing nobody inside the organization will notice is missing."

**Tagline.** A pithy phrase characterizing the whole enterprise: what it is and what makes it great, right below/above/next to the Site ID — the one place users expect a concise statement of purpose. Good taglines are clear and informative (explain exactly what you do); just long enough — six to eight words; convey differentiation and a clear benefit (Nielsen: a really good tagline is one no one else in the world could use); personable, lively, sometimes clever — clever only if it conveys rather than obscures the benefit. Bad taglines sound generic. A motto ("We bring good things to life," "You're in good hands," "To protect and to serve") expresses an ideal; a tagline conveys a value proposition — a motto won't tell me what the thing is (a public utility like NationalGrid can get away with a motto; captive audience). Sites that can skip it: household words, famous offline brands — but even they benefit, since the online mission is never exactly the offline one.

**The fifth question — where do I start?** After a quick look I should be able to say: here's where to start if I want to search; to browse; to sample their best stuff. Process sites (mortgage application): the entry point should leap out. Registration/sign-in prominent. Promos yelling "Start here!" obscure entry points; fix by making entry points look like what they are (search box looks like a search box; section list looks like a list) and labeling them: "Search," "Browse by Category," "Sign in," "Start here."

**Killing the golden goose (tragedy of the commons).** Promotion on Home works too well; every stakeholder wants one. Rewards go to the promoted section; the cost (a more cluttered, less effective Home page) is shared by all — so each rational stakeholder adds another and the commons is destroyed. It happens gradually: "just...one...more...thing." Requires constant vigilance; educate stakeholders about overgrazing; alternatives: cross-promote from other popular pages, take turns using the same Home slot.

## 8. Religious debates and the Average User (ch. 8)

**Religious debates.** Endless team arguments about usability: strongly held personal beliefs about things that can't be proven; nobody changes their mind; they waste time, create tension, erode respect and block critical decisions.

**Forces that cause them.** (1) "Everybody likes ___": we're all users with strong likes/dislikes and we project them onto users in general — "most users are like us" (those who differ aren't "sensible people"). (2) Farmers vs. cowmen — professional passion: designers get visceral pleasure from elegant type and subtle cues; developers enjoy complexity and ingenious features; endorphins make it hard to imagine others feel differently; plus the hype culture (management, marketing, business development making promises) vs. the craft culture (designers, developers delivering them), producing arbitrary edicts ("it came to our CEO in a dream"). (3) The myth of the Average User: stalemates lead to seeking what "most users like" via experts, research, surveys, focus groups — but there is no Average User.

**"All Web users are unique and all Web use is basically idiosyncratic."** Watching users shows reactions depend on so many variables that one-dimensional likes/dislikes are futile and counter-productive. The myth's worst effect: it makes design look like finding out what people like (pull-downs good or bad; long page vs. many short; carousels, mega menus, rollovers). There are no simple right answers to the important questions; "what works is good, integrated design that fills a need — carefully thought out, well executed, and tested." Some things are clearly wrong, but they're not what teams argue about.

**The antidote.** Replace "Do most people like pull-down menus?" with "Does this pull-down, with these items and this wording in this context on this page create a good experience for most people who are likely to use this site?" Only testing answers that: build some version (even crude), watch people try to figure out what it is and how to use it. Testing moves the discussion from right/wrong and like/dislike to works/doesn't work, and shows how varied users are.

## 9. Usability testing principles (ch. 9; procedures in usability-test-kit.md)

**Too little, too late, for the wrong reasons.** "Launching in two weeks + usability testing" = a disaster check; "two months" = settling an aesthetic argument. Testing usually reveals the argued-about things didn't matter: "test to decide which color drapes are best, only to learn that they forgot to put windows in the room" (cascading vs. mega menus is irrelevant if nobody understands the value proposition).

**Focus groups are not usability tests.** Focus group: 5–10 people around a table talking about opinions, past experiences, reactions to concepts; good for feelings and opinions in the abstract — is the idea sensible, is the value proposition attractive, how do people solve the problem today, how do they feel about you and competitors; belongs in planning, before design. Usability test: watch *one person at a time* try to *use* something (site, prototype, sketches) on typical tasks to find what confuses or frustrates; used throughout the project. "You watch people actually use things, instead of just listening to them talk about them."

**Several true things.** If you want a great site you've got to test — after a few weeks you can't see it freshly; testing is like friends visiting from out of town: you notice your hometown and realize what you take for granted isn't obvious. Testing one user is 100% better than none — even the worst test with the wrong user shows important things (a 15-minute workshop demo produces pages of notes; one change saved a team $100,000). Testing one user early is better than 50 near the end — making testing a big deal means it happens too late; changes to a live site are not easy (users resist change, simple changes have far-reaching effects).

**Participants, not subjects** — we test the site, not them.

**Discount history.** Labs with one-way mirrors and statistical samples: $20,000–$50,000 per study. Nielsen's 1989 "Usability Engineering at a Discount": no lab, far fewer participants, $5,000–$10,000 per round — still too expensive to happen often. DIY testing: no time, no money. If you can afford a professional, hire one; otherwise do it yourself (full method in *Rocket Surgery Made Easy*).

**Qualitative, not proof.** Three users can't prove anything and won't find every problem — true and irrelevant: the purpose is actionable insight, not proof (proof needs quantitative tests with large samples and rigid protocols). "You can find more problems in half a day than you can fix in a month," so finding all problems wouldn't help; three users hit many of the most significant problems for the tasks tested; more rounds beat wringing more from one round.

**Recruit loosely and grade on a curve.** Narrow profiles cost time and money and reduce the amount of testing; early on, almost anyone will hit real problems. Ask of each problem: "Would our users have that problem, or was it only a problem because they didn't know what our users know?" If domain knowledge is required, recruit some with it, not all. Always include non-target participants: don't design so only your target can use it (some experts won't know your terminology; you must support novices anyway); "we're all beginners under the skin" — experts muddle through at a higher level; experts are rarely insulted by true clarity (not dumbing down).

**Typical problems found.** Unclear on the concept (don't get it, or think they do and are wrong); the words they're looking for aren't there (you didn't anticipate, or your words aren't theirs); too much going on (it's there but unseen — reduce noise or turn up the volume so it pops from the hierarchy).

**Fix the most serious problems first.** Serious problems often go unfixed ("that's all going to change soon"; low-hanging fruit chosen over one hard problem) — why big, well-funded sites keep serious problems. Maxim: "Focus ruthlessly on fixing the most serious problems first." A fix need not be perfect or complete — just enough to take it out of the "serious" category. Resist adding things (first instinct is an explanation; often the right fix is removing what obscures). Distrust feature requests ("now that I think of it, I probably wouldn't use that"; a truly great idea triggers "why didn't we think of that?!"). Ignore kayak problems (momentary wrong turn, noticed quickly, recovered unaided, not fazed; "if the user's second guess is always right, that's good enough").

## 10. Mobile (ch. 10)

**What's different.** Basic principles are the same; people move faster and read even less on small screens. Mobile design is still "Wild West"; Krug avoids specific best practices (conventions haven't settled) and states what will stay true.

**It's all about tradeoffs.** Design = constraints + tradeoffs; constraints can focus (a sofa that must fit this space). "Many — if not most — serious usability problems are the result of a poor decision about a tradeoff." (CBS News mobile: tiny story chunks, slow loads, scroll past the same photo, repeat 8 times — presumably page views or CMS segmentation outweighed user experience.)

**The itty-bitty living space.** Subset mobile sites raise "which parts do we leave out?" Mobile First (design the mobile version on the most important features, then add for desktop) is great for forcing prioritization, but the interpretation "include only what people do on the move" was wrong: people use phones on the couch and want everything (everyone wants some things; summed, that's everything). So prioritize harder: things used in a hurry or frequently close at hand; everything else a few taps away with an obvious path. Mobile sites go deeper (3–5 levels); more tapping/scrolling is fine as long as the user stays confident the target is further down or behind that link. Maxim: "Managing real estate challenges shouldn't be done at the cost of usability."

**Scalable/responsive design** (dynamic, fluid, adaptive, responsive): lots of work and very hard to do well; now mandatory because small screens dominate; separate versions = "two sets of books" → double effort, out of sync. Meanwhile: allow zooming (don't resist mobile viewing); don't leave me standing at the front door (a link from email/social must open the article, not the mobile Home page); always provide a link to the full site (convention: Mobile Site/Full Site toggle at the bottom of every page; people will zoom for features they need; 7" tablets in landscape may prefer desktop pages).

**Affordances.** Visual clues in an object's design that suggest how to use it (Norman 1988, now "signifiers"; Krug keeps "affordances"). A 3-D button says click; a bordered rectangle says type here. Clearer cues = less ambiguity. They must be noticeable; mobile has made many less noticeable or invisible — "affordances are the last thing you should hide." Not everything in your face, just visible enough to notice the ones you need.

**No cursor = no hover = no clue.** Capacitive screens sense touch, not hovering; no cursor; tooltips, hover-state buttons and hover-revealed menus don't exist for mobile users. Find replacements.

**Flat design: friend or foe?** Affordances need visual distinctions; flat design removes them, trading a clean look for the information textured elements carried. Distinctions are multi-dimensional (position in the nav bar plus formatting such as reversed type or all caps). Flat "has sucked the air out of the room." Rule: do flat if you must, but use all remaining dimensions to compensate for what you lose.

**Speed.** Can never be too fast; slow = frustration and lost goodwill (AP app loads a wall of photos before showing the alert — Krug now opens NYT/Google News instead). Mobile speeds are unreliable off Wi-Fi; responsive solutions must not ship huge code and oversized images.

**Delight.** Words people use: fun, surprising, impressive, captivating, clever, magical ("something you'd have been burned at the stake for a few hundred years ago"). Comes from marrying something people would love but don't imagine is possible with a bright idea about new technology (SoundHound identifies a song and scrolls synced lyrics; Paper: five tools, no options, each tuned to look good). In a crowded app market you must do something incredibly well; delight is "extra credit" — don't pursue it so hard you forget usability.

**Learnability.** Apps with more than a few features, especially unconventional ones, are often hard to learn; typical help is one or two launch screens you can't find again, or a link to a developer site. Clear (to-do app) has a ten-screen tour and a tutorial that is itself a list — yet in Krug's live demos nobody completed "create a list called Chicago trip with three items": they never grasp the three levels (lists, items, settings) or how to move between them, and can't reach Help without that — Catch-22. Best seller anyway; but how many bought it and never mastered it? Usability testing shows where learning fails.

**Memorability.** Will you remember how to use it next time? Best approach: make it incredibly clear and easy to learn the first time. ASketch hides all controls to maximize canvas; for months Krug couldn't remember how to start a new drawing (tried double/triple taps, edge taps, swipes, multi-finger). People will invest effort once, not twice; unless very impressed they abandon — the fate of most apps. "Life is cheap (99 cents) on mobile devices."

**Mobile testing.** Same process as desktop; differences are logistics (see usability-test-kit.md §9).

## 11. Goodwill and courtesy (ch. 11)

**Two components of usability.** Clarity ("do they get it?") and doing the right thing — being considerate. Ask: "Does my site behave like a mensch?" (a person of integrity who does the right thing). Case: an airline on strike-deadline day with nothing about the strike anywhere; the only real FAQ was "What's going to happen to me?"

**The reservoir of goodwill.** Every visitor arrives with a reservoir; each problem lowers it; exhaust it and they leave — or come back less eagerly, think less of you, savage you on social media, lower your NPS. Properties: idiosyncratic (some people have a big reserve, some small — don't count on a large one); situational (a hurry or a bad prior experience lowers it before arrival); refillable (acts that show you're looking out for them); a single mistake can empty it (a registration form with tons of fields).

**Things that diminish goodwill.** Hiding information I want (support phone numbers, shipping rates, prices; a visible 800 number keeps people on the site longer trying to solve it themselves; airport Wi-Fi hides cost three pages deep). Punishing me for not doing things your way (dashes in SSNs, spaces in credit cards — spaces make typing easier — parentheses in phone numbers: "don't make me jump through hoops because you don't want to write a little bit of code"). Asking for information you don't really need. Shucking and jiving (faux sincerity: "Your call is important to us"). Putting sizzle in my way (pages bloated with feel-good marketing photos). Looking amateurish (sloppy, disorganized) — though almost nobody leaves over looks; ignore color comments in tests unless three of four users say "puke." Deliberate choices (pop-ups for +10% revenue) can be business decisions — make them informed, not inadvertent.

**Things that increase goodwill.** Know the three main things people want to do and make them obvious and easy (teams agree on the three; they just don't prioritize them — "nothing should get in the way of applying for a mortgage"). Tell me what I want to know (shipping, hotel parking fees, outages — candor earns points). Save me steps (receipt email with a link that submits the tracking number). Put effort into it (HP support: generate, verify, present, organize the information → Krug keeps buying HP). Know the questions I'll have and answer them — FAQs that are real (not QWWPWAs), kept current (support's top five questions this week at the top of the Support page), and candid. Creature comforts (printer-friendly pages: drop ads, keep figures). Make it easy to recover from errors (testing prevents many; otherwise a graceful, obvious way back). When in doubt, apologize (if you can't do what they want, show that you know you're inconveniencing them).

## 12. Accessibility (ch. 12)

**Position.** Unless people with disabilities aren't your audience, a site isn't usable unless it's accessible. Krug's three-second test: increase the browser text size (Text Size, not Zoom — only sites that moved beyond fixed fonts respond); almost every site fails.

**Why the usual arguments fail with developers.** "___% of the population has a disability" sounds like advocacy exaggeration to able-bodied 22-year-olds; "it benefits everyone" is always argued with closed captioning alone (the "Tang" argument), and developers can imagine adaptations making things worse for everyone else. The one reason that matters: it's profoundly the right thing to do — blind people can now read almost any newspaper on their own. Plus a legislative stick is coming.

**Fears.** More work (an "initiative" with reports and task forces); compromised design — "buttered cats": places where good design for people with disabilities and for everyone else are in direct opposition. Ideal: the Chicago taxi sign with a Plexiglas Braille overlay — each audience gets the best experience, nothing halved.

**It is complicated.** Validators behave like grammar checkers, not spell checkers: a few clear fixes (missing alt text) plus vague warnings and long maybe-lists — discouraging. Tools, screen readers and processes must improve; that's no excuse.

**Four things to do right now.** (1) Fix the usability problems that confuse everyone — the reverse of Tang: usability for the rest of us is one of the most effective accessibility measures; whatever confuses most people confuses disabled users, who recover with more difficulty (imagine a confusing form error without seeing the page); test often; guidelines applied to an unclear site are lipstick on a pig. (2) Read Theofanos & Redish, "Guidelines for Accessible and Usable Web Sites: Observing Users Who Work with Screen Readers" (16 blind users; 20 minutes): screen-reader users "scan with their ears" — impatient, fast voice rates, listen to the first few words of a link or line and move on; a keyword not at the start of a link/line may never be heard. Ideally also spend a few hours watching one or two blind users. (3) Read a book: Horton & Quesenbery, *A Web for Everyone* ("good UX equals good accessibility"); Thatcher et al., *Web Accessibility* (laws and regulations). (4) Low-hanging fruit: alt text on every image (null `alt=""` for decorative images; descriptive otherwise; webaim.org for technique); correct heading elements (h1 page title, h2 major sections, h3 subheads; style with CSS); `<label>` for every form field; "Skip to Main Content" link at the start of each page; everything reachable by keyboard; significant text/background contrast (never light grey on dark grey); accessible templates/themes.

## 13. Making usability happen (ch. 13)

**The territory.** Late 1990s: Usability and Information Architecture under "User Centered Design." Now the UX umbrella holds a dozen specialties (interaction, interface, visual design, content management, usability, IA); UCD = right product + usable; UX = users' needs at every stage of the product life cycle (ad → purchase → delivery → return). Jobs/Ive made the business case, so usability is an easier sell; but many siblings compete and few understand the differences. When someone says "it's all UX now," ask how they learn about users, how they test whether people can use what they build, and how they get changes to happen; if they don't, they need your help; if they do, learn from them.

**The usual advice** (fine if you can): demonstrate ROI (Bias & Mayhew, *Cost-Justifying Usability*; "changing the label increased sales by 0.25%" — laborious and contestable); speak their language (pain points, touch points, KPIs).

**Krug's tactics.** Get your boss (and her boss) to watch a usability test in person — the tactic that works best; invite them to "poke their head in for team morale"; they stay longer, because it's the first time they've seen anyone use the company's site. Live beats a recap like a live game beats the evening news; second best: highlight clips in presentations or a <3-minute clip on the intranet with an intriguing email. Do the first test on your own time, without permission, with volunteers; pick an easy target with one serious, quickly fixable problem (a poorly labeled button); test, fix, publicize; measure if easy (support calls before/after). Test the competition — everyone loves it, nobody has anything on the line; a great brown-bag event. Empathize with management — genuinely understand their position. Know your place — humility; share what you know rather than tell people how things should be done. Books: Tomer Sharon, *It's Our Research*; Leah Buley, *The User Experience Team of One* (ch. 3–4).

**Resist the dark forces.** Usability is user advocacy (the Lorax). Influencing is fine (Cialdini, Weinschenk) and persuasion is fine if not deceptive; think-aloud can show why persuasion works. But usability tests can't measure desirability (only "a sense"; that's market research). Refuse manipulation: hidden pre-checked newsletter boxes (benign-ish), fake "Start Download" buttons that install toolbars and reset Home/search (darker), phishing/scams (black hat). "It's not part of your job. The users are counting on you."

## 14. Definitive always/never rules (ch. 13)

1. Don't use small, low-contrast type. (Large low-contrast or small high-contrast are tolerable; avoid them too.)
2. Don't put labels inside form fields — unless all of these hold: the form is exceptionally simple; labels disappear when typing starts and reappear if the field is emptied; labels can never be confused with answers; there is no possibility of submitting the label with the input ("Job TiAssistant Managertle"); and it's fully accessible. (See "Don't Put Labels Inside Text Boxes (Unless You're Luke W).")
3. Preserve the distinction between visited and unvisited links — tracked by URL, so "Book a flight" shows as visited after you clicked "Book a trip" to the same page; any colors, as long as noticeably different.
4. Don't float headings between paragraphs — closer to the following text than the preceding.

Closing caveat: rules may be bent (some sites *want* to puzzle people) — know which rule you're bending and have a good reason.
