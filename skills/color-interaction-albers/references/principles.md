## 1. Foundational stance

**Color is the most relative medium in art** (Intro, ch. IV, XXV). "In visual perception a color is
almost never seen as it really is — as it physically is." Therefore "color deceives continually."
Everything else in the book is a consequence. *Apply:* never judge a color in isolation; judge it
where it will actually sit, next to what it will actually sit next to.

**Physical fact vs. psychic effect** (Intro, ch. IV, XX). Experience shows a discrepancy between what
is physically present and what is perceived. The haptic analogy: three pots (warm / lukewarm /
cold); after the hands have been in the outer pots, the lukewarm pot feels cold to one hand and
warm to the other. Optical illusions deceive the same way.

**Factual fact vs. actual fact** (ch. XXV terms). Wave-length data from optical analysis is a
*factual fact* — something that remains what it is. Seeing opaque color as transparent, 3 colors as
4 or as 2, flat colors as intersecting or fluted, a single contour doubled, vibrating or vanishing —
these are *actual facts*. "Actual" relates to "action": not fixed, changing with time. Color *acts*:
"to change by giving up, by losing identity", as a real actor can play Henry VIII and then Henry IX.
*Apply:* a hex value is a factual fact; what the user sees is an actual fact. Design for the latter.

**Interaction / interdependence** (ch. XXV terms). "The mutual influencing of colors we call —
interaction. Seen from the opposite viewpoint, it is — interdependence." It extends across senses:
a color changes visually when a changing tone is heard simultaneously; food colors and container
colors change appetite.

**Practice before theory** (Intro, ch. XXIV, XXV). The course reverses the academic order: theory
"is the conclusion of practice." Just as knowledge of acoustics does not make one musical, "no
color system by itself can develop one's sensitivity for color." Color systems are taught at the
*end*, when "eyes and mind are — after productive exercises — better prepared and more receptive."

**What counts is seeing** (Intro). "What counts here — first and last — is not so-called knowledge
of so-called facts, but vision — seeing." Seeing = *Schauen* (as in Weltanschauung), coupled with
fantasy and imagination. General training = "development of observation and articulation."

**The what vs. the how** (ch. II, quoting Kandinsky). Factual identification of colors in a painting
has nothing to do with sensitive seeing of the color action. "What counts is not the what but the
how." We can hear a single tone but "almost never see a single color unconnected and unrelated";
colors are "in continuous flux, constantly related to changing neighbors and changing conditions."

**Visual memory is poor; color vocabulary is poorer** (ch. I). Say "red" to 50 people and there are
50 reds. Even with the same retinal projection no one can be sure of the same perception. "It is
hard, if not impossible, to remember distinct colors"; auditory memory can repeat a melody heard
once or twice, visual memory cannot do the equivalent. Daily vocabulary has only about 30 color
names for innumerable colors. *Apply:* never rely on remembered colors or verbal color names
("the blue") — compare samples side by side, under the same conditions.

**Reading depends on context** (ch. II). We read words as whole "word pictures", not letters; more
differentiated letters read more easily; all-capital words are hardest (equal height, volume,
width); Albers judges sans-serif text to give "uneasy reading" because sans-serifs were designed
for captions and produce poor word pictures. Music is heard in "the in-between of the tones, of
their placing and of their spacing." The lesson transfers: clear reading of color depends on
recognizing context.

**Interdependence of color with form, placement, quantity, quality, pronouncement** (Intro). The
study moves from color–color interaction to color's dependence on: *form and placement*;
*quantity* (amount: extension in area and/or number, including *recurrence*); *quality* (intensity
of light and/or hue); *pronouncement* (separating or connecting boundaries).

## 2. Relativity and influence

**Influence: influencing vs. influenced color** (ch. IV). "Change is a result of influence." The
*influencing* color (the ground) is distinguished from the *influenced* color (the small figure).
Some colors are hard to change, others susceptible; some are inclined to exert influence, others to
accept it. *Conditions for a strong effect:* small amounts of the influenced color on *large*
grounds; grounds alike in size and shape so that only the grounds differ factually; influence from
one direction only.

**Two directions of influence: light and hue** (ch. IV, VII). "There are 2 kinds of changing
influences working in 2 directions, light on the one side and hue on the other. And both occur
simultaneously though in varying strength." Color differences are caused by these 2 factors, "in
most cases by both at the same time."

**The more different the grounds, the stronger the changing influence** (ch. VII).

**Subtraction of color** (ch. VII). "Any ground subtracts its own hue from colors which it carries
and therefore influences." Likewise "the light of a ground subtracts in the same way that its hue
does" (light colors on light grounds, dark on dark). Therefore "any diversion among colors in hue as
well as in light-dark relationship can be reduced if not obliterated visually on grounds of equal
qualities." Demonstration: 3 reds on white simply read "red"; on another red their hue and light
differences become obvious; on a red equal to one of them, only 2 "show" — the third is absorbed.

**Pushing** (ch. VII). By contrast one can "push" the light and/or hue of a color away from its
first appearance toward the opposite qualities — which amounts to *adding* opposite qualities. The
parallel route is *subtracting* undesired qualities via a ground of equal quality.

**The middle color / "topologically in the middle"** (ch. VI). To make 1 color show 2 faces that
echo 2 reversed grounds (3 colors look like 2), "the only fitting color is the one which is
topologically in the middle of the colors of the 2 grounds." If the collection has no such paper,
change one or both grounds instead of pushing the in-between color to one side. Easy when grounds
share a hue (lighter/darker green); harder across hues; most interesting with complementary grounds.

**Pitfalls Albers names** (ch. IV):
- Small pieces on small grounds destroy the effect.
- Interlocked tile patterns annul the effects because of (a) simultaneous influence from too many
  directions (left, right, above, below) and (b) unfavorable distribution of area between
  influencing and influenced color. Such presentation "lacks both sight and insight."
- Pleasant or harmonious combination "is unimportant" in these studies; precision and clean
  execution are required.

**Relativity (term)** (ch. XXV). Caused by "a variance of measure, by lack or avoidance of standard
rules, or by changing viewpoints"; one phenomenon then has varying readings. A light grey "may look
dark at one time and almost white at another, and at various times like a shade or a tint of any
color, as green may look reddish."

## 3. After-image / simultaneous contrast — the root cause

**Definition** (ch. VIII). After staring at red for up to half a minute and shifting to white, "all
normal eyes suddenly see green or blue-green instead of white" — the complement. "The phenomenon of
seeing green (in this case) instead of white is called after-image, or simultaneous contrast." A
plausible explanation: retinal receptors tuned to 3 primaries (red, yellow, blue); staring fatigues
the red-sensitive parts, so white then yields only yellow + blue = green.

**It is the cause of all color deception** (Preface, ch. VIII, XV). "All these and similar color
deceptions result from 1 phenomenon, the 'after-image.'" Pulling/pushing of colors, reversed
grounds, subtraction, fluting, vibrating and vanishing boundaries all trace to it.

**Contrast reversal / double after-image** (ch. VIII). Staring at yellow circles filling a white
square, then shifting to an empty white square, one sees not blue (the complement) but yellow
*diamond* shapes — the leftover shapes between the circles. "A double and thus reversed
after-image, sometimes called contrast reversal."

**Nobody is immune** (ch. VIII, Preface). After-image "is a psycho-physiological phenomenon" so "no
normal eye, not even the most trained one, is foolproof against color deception. He who claims to
see colors independent of their illusionary changes fools only himself, and no one else."
*Apply:* do not trust a reviewer (or yourself) who says "I can see they are the same color" while
the surroundings differ; isolate the samples (peepholes) to check.

**Complementary = a color accompanied by its after-image** (ch. XVI). But complementaries are
"topographically quite vague": the complement of a specific color looks different in different
systems.

## 4. Light intensity and color intensity

**Two decisive characteristics of any color** (ch. XIV): *color intensity (brightness)* and *light
intensity (lightness)*. Every color interval therefore has this duality.

**"Light intensity" instead of "value"** (ch. V, XXIII, XXV terms). "Value" is a distinct measure
only in the Munsell system (value = lightness); elsewhere it has no outspoken meaning, and careless
use for "equal lightness" plus false examples in books "has destroyed it as a means of measure."
Albers uses "light intensity" as self-explanatory. *Apply:* when someone says two colors are "equal
value", treat it as unverified until tested.

**The darker color** (ch. V): "visually the heavier one, or the one containing more black, or less
white."

**Most people cannot judge lighter/darker across hues** (ch. V). In Albers' self-test with pairs of
different hues (never of equal light intensity), results stayed constant for years: **60% of answers
wrong, 40% right**, undecided not counted — including advanced painting students. Only a minority
can distinguish lighter from darker "within close intervals when obscured by contrasting hues or by
different color intensities." Abstaining in doubt is encouraged: "not voting can have a positive
meaning."

**Agreement is possible on lightness, rarely on brightness** (ch. V, XIV, XXI). After training a
class can agree on light intensity; on color intensity ("which among a number of reds is the
reddest red") agreement is rare in a group. Lightness is a factual measure; brightness is "an
interpretation of illusions" with contradictory readings. Hence transformation exercises work
mainly with light intensity.

**Photography is not proof of light relationship** (ch. V). Black-and-white photography "registers
all lights lighter and all darks darker than the more adjustable eye"; middle greys are flattened
or lost. The eye has scotopic (low-light) seeing in addition to photopic. Color photography deviates
more: blue and red are overemphasized, whites look greenish. A "higher key" reproduction can lose
whole elements (the Ensor example). *Apply (extractor note):* a screenshot, a print, or a device
with different gamma is not a neutral judge of your lightness relationships — check on the target
medium.

**Preferences are prejudices** (ch. V, XVIII). Everyone has color preferences and aversions, which
shift over time. Recognize which colors dominate your work and which you reject; "usually a special
effort in using disliked colors ends with our falling in love with them." Preferences and dislikes
"usually result from prejudices, from lack of experience and insight."

**Equal light intensity is rare and demands patience** (ch. XXIII). "Very few people — including
many colorists and painters — have ever seen 2 adjacent colors of true equal light value." Several
classes could not produce one convincing pair.

## 5. Mixture, transparence and space-illusion

**Illusionary mixture** (ch. IX). With opaque paper nothing is mixed physically; mixture is studied
"in our imagination." Two "color parents" and one "color descendant": the descendant placed between
them in overlapping shapes reads as their mixture. "Any 2 colors can produce many mixtures."
Backward inference is also possible: from a mixture and one parent, guess the other.

**Illusion of transparence** (ch. IX). In an illusionary mixture "1 color seems to show through the
other" — the mixture paper "loses its opacity and appears transparent or translucent." *Condition:*
the colors must be placed in *overlapping shapes*; the overlap is "the logical place for the
mixture." For a more intensive effect keep the mixture area *larger* than the two mixing areas.

**Three discoveries about mixture** (ch. XI):
1. Under normal conditions a subtractive mixture is "not as light as the lighter of the color
   parents nor as dark as the darker one", and reciprocally neither higher nor lower in color
   intensity than its parents.
2. A mixture "depends upon the proportion in which colors are mixed" — nearness to one parent
   "necessitates distance from the opposite side." A parent may predominate.
3. When one color reads as above or below another, a third deception appears: *space-illusion*.

**Additive vs. subtractive (factual) mixture** (ch. X). (a) Direct, projected light: every
overlapping mixture is lighter than any parent; the sum of all colors is white (additive).
(b) Reflected light from pigment: mixing never yields white; "the more color that is mixed, the more
the mixture approaches a dark grey leaning toward black" (subtractive). Optical mixture on a rotating
wheel loses less light than mechanical mixing — its sum approaches middle grey, not dark grey — but
it still cannot be lighter than the lighter parent. "Mixtures gain light only in direct color,
whereas mixtures of reflected colors lose light." *(extractor note: screens are direct/projected
light; printed material is reflected.)*

**Middle mixture** (ch. XI, XV). The "most significant and the most difficult mixture":
equidistant — "in light and in hue" — from either parent, i.e. with no predominance of either
parent. It "demands precise placement". Corrective logic (bar diagrams): if the middle is too light,
look for a darker middle; if unavailable, adjust the outer colors instead (raise the lower, lower
the upper) — "another way of correct placement."

**Behavior of a middle mixture** (ch. XI). With a true middle mixture "all boundaries are equally
soft or hard. As a consequence, a middle mixture appears frontal, as a color by itself"; it "will
behave unspatially, unless its own shape, or surrounding shapes, decides differently" — comparable
to any symmetrical order. An untrained eye finds equidistance hard to recognize; boundaries are the
helpful measure.

**Intersecting colors, fluting, illusion of volume** (ch. XV, Preface). When a strip of the middle
color widens between its lighter and darker parents, it reads "not as 1 but as 2 colors", lighter
at the edge next to the dark parent and darker at the edge next to the light one: "the middle color
plays the role of both mixture parents, presenting them in reversed placement." In a true middle
mixture the parents appear in equal amounts; usually one reveals predominance. This reading of
constituents produces a new deception, "an illusion of volume" — the *fluting effect* of Doric
column channeling. Albers calls this *color interpenetration* (Preface, XV-2).

## 6. Boundaries

**Boundaries measure distance** (ch. XI). "Distance, nearness, and equidistance between colors can be
recognized through the boundaries between the mixture and the mixture parents." Comparing and
distinguishing boundaries gives "a new and important measure for the reading of the plastic action
of color, that is, for the spatial organization of color."

**Soft = near/connected; hard = distant/separated** (ch. XI). "Softer boundaries disclose nearness
implying connection, harder boundaries indicate distance, separation." In both cases colors are
read as above/below or in front/behind — "here and there", "over and beyond" — i.e. in space.

**Pronouncement** (Intro). The study ends with the interdependence of color "with pronouncement (by
separating or connecting boundaries)".

**Cézanne's lesson** (ch. XI). He was "the first to develop color areas which produce both distinct
and indistinct endings — areas connected and unconnected — areas with and without boundaries — as
means of plastic organization", and "to prevent evenly painted areas from looking flat and frontal,
he used emphasized borders sparingly, mainly where he needed a spatial separation."

**Boundaries reveal progressions** (ch. XX). In an arithmetic series of contiguous layers "the
boundaries gradually become softer, whereas in a geometric order the boundaries remain equally
distinct." In the swimming pool, step edges soften downward for two reasons: added blueness reduces
light (edges less clear) and the increase itself decreases (less contrast between neighbors).

**Vibrating boundaries / enforced contours** (ch. XXII). *Conditions:* colors "contrasting in their
hues but also close or similar in light intensity." Effects: additional illusionary colors hard to
define in hue, appearing as shadow on one side of the boundary and reflected light on the other; or
a duplication/triplication of the boundary line. Perceived by some people and not others; varies
with glasses and near/far focus. "This initially exciting effect also feels aggressive and often
even uncomfortable to our eyes." Rarely used "except for a screaming effect in advertising"; as a
result "unpleasant, disliked, and avoided."

**Vanishing boundaries** (ch. XXIII, Preface). Boundaries between colors "can be made nearly
unrecognizable, or made practically invisible — through the choice of color alone." *Conditions:*
opposite of vibrating boundaries; "not possible between very contrasting hues"; "confined to
adjacent, neighboring colors"; "depends most decisively on equal 'light intensity'" — real equality
in lightness or darkness. Nature's example: the shaded grey underside of cumulus clouds against the
nearby blue sky (sun at your back). Reproduction warning: photomechanical processes destroy such
delicacies. *Apply:* this is both the rarest effect to achieve and the commonest accidental failure
(figure dissolving into ground).

## 7. Optical mixture and the Bezold effect

**Optical mixture** (ch. XII). Distinct from after-image: instead of colors pulling/pushing each
other "toward both greater difference and greater similarity", 2 or more colors perceived
simultaneously are "seen combined and thus merged into 1 new color"; the originals are "annulled
and made invisible" and replaced by a substitute. Impressionists/Pointillists applied yellow and blue
unmixed in small dots; that the dots were small "indicates that this effect depends on size and on
distance." Same principle underlies 3-/4-color process printing and halftone (black dots + white
paper -> tones of grey).

**Bezold effect** (ch. XIII). A special optical mixture named after Wilhelm von Bezold (1837–1907),
who found he could change the color combinations of his rug designs "entirely by adding or changing
1 color only." Albers notes "there is so far no clear recognition of the optical-perceptual
conditions involved." *Apply:* changing one recurring color (e.g. a line/outline/background weave)
can re-tint an entire composition.

## 8. Intervals and transformation

**Color intervals** (ch. XIV). As a 4-note tune keeps its character in any key because its intervals
("acoustical constellation, comparable with a topographic relationship") remain the same, one can
speak of intervals between colors, with the duality brightness/lightness. Because agreement exists
on lightness only, transformation exercises concern light intensity.

**Transformation / parallel intervals** (ch. XIV). A group of 4 equal squares forms a constellation
(pairs connect or separate as vertical, horizontal, diagonal pairs, or a trio against one). The task
is to transfer the relationships "to a higher or lower key". Limits: if the group contains the
darkest color available one cannot go lower; the lightest white permits no higher key. Usually one
cannot keep the original 4; if 4 equally raised/lowered colors cannot be found, "the original set
should be changed." Success: "both groups should show equal relationships in equal placement, as a
constellation", and "the boundaries between the 4 rectangles will also appear similar in both
groups." The aim "is not to present a pleasant, perhaps harmonious look, but ... one distinct
relationship — parallel intervals."

**Compare by superimposition, never across a gap** (ch. XIV, V). It is "poor 'psychological
engineering'" to present gradation steps separated by black lines; two separated groups cannot be
compared accurately. "The only way in which the 2 groups can be compared easily and accurately is to
superimpose 1 group on the other" — exchange a small central rectangle between the two groups.

**Small steps produce film color** (ch. XIV). "By keeping the stepping — up or down — small, a
special effect of transparence is achieved called film color."

## 9. Harmony, quantity, placement, recurrence

**Harmony rules are "worn out"** (ch. XVI). Systems claim that certain constellations
(complementaries, split complementaries, triads, tetrads, octads) provide harmony and that harmony
is the aim of juxtaposition. But their "deflections, such as incomplete triads and incomplete
tetrads, indicate that their measure is only arbitrary"; a triad of one system hardly fits another.
Conclusion: "we may forget for a while those rules of thumb of complementaries, whether complete or
'split,' and of triads and tetrads as well. They are worn out." And "no mechanical color system is
flexible enough to precalculate the manifold changing factors ... in a single prescribed recipe."

**Why harmony illustrations mislead** (ch. XVI). They are presented "in a most theoretical and least
practicable manner": all members "in the same quantity and the same shape, as well as in the same
number (just once) and sometimes even in similar light intensity." This outer equalization unifies
"at the expense of the more important inner relatedness — namely, as color only."

**Factors that change an applied color scheme** (ch. XVI). Beyond quantity, form and recurrence:
"changed and changing light — and, even worse, several simultaneous lights; reflection of lights
and of colors; direction and sequence of reading; presentation in varying materials; constant or
altering juxtaposition of related and unrelated objects." The "ideal" combination "often appears
changed, lost, and reversed."

**Cooking analogy** (ch. XVI). "Even a good cooking recipe demands tasting and repeated tasting
while it is being followed. And the best tasting still depends on a cook with taste."

**Dissonance is as desirable as consonance** (ch. XVI, XXIV). Giving up a preference for harmony,
"we accept dissonance to be as desirable as consonance." Besides balance through harmony
(comparable to symmetry) "there is equilibrium possible between color tensions, related to a more
dynamic asymmetry." Aim: "flexible imagination, discovery, invention — taste."

**Color vs. tone** (ch. XVI). Tones live in time, one direction, and vanish; colors are "connected
predominantly in space", seen "in any direction and at any speed", and remain, so one can return to
them. Applied color is additionally "characterized by shape and size, by recurrence and placement"
(shape and size do not apply to tones) — so color composition defies notation.

**Any reflected color contains all other colors** (ch. XVI). A spectrograph shows reflected color
"contains all visible wave lengths" — "any reflected color — not just white — consists of all other
colors" (visible in single plates of 4-color reproduction, each showing a complete picture).

**Quantity is a quality** (ch. XVI). Quantity "not only designates amounts, as of weight or number,
but also is a means of underlining, of pronouncement, and a means of equilibrium, of balance." Two
quantity questions: *how much* (size — extension in area) and *how often* (recurrence — extension
in number). "Both measurements concern predominance and emphasis. They establish weight in space —
and weight in time."

**Schopenhauer's quantities** (ch. XVI, XXIV). Reworking Goethe's 6-part circle: yellow (lightest)
gets the smallest area, violet (darkest) the largest. Pairs yellow–violet, blue–orange, red–green
each get a third of the ring, subdivided 1/4+3/4, 1/3+2/3, 1/2+1/2 — in twelfths 3:9, 4:8, 6:6;
around the circle from yellow to green: **3 : 4 : 6 : 9 : 8 : 6**. Of modern systems only Munsell
calculates quantity by area extension — and none counts recurrence.

**Any color works with any other color — given appropriate quantities** (ch. XVI). "Independent of
harmony rules, any color 'goes' or 'works' with any other color, presupposing that their quantities
are appropriate." There are no comprehensive rules for this ("we feel fortunate").

**Cast and performance** (ch. XVI). A set of 4 colors = "actors"/"cast"; 4 arrangements =
"performances" within an unchanging "stage"; by changes in quantity alone — "which result in shifts
of dominance, of recurrence, and consequently of placement" — the same cast reads as 4 different
casts, changing "climate or temperature, tempo or rhythm ... atmosphere or mood." Essential
question: "which group of colors is ready to lose its identity as a cast?"

**Amount reduces distance** (ch. XVI). A discovery of contemporary painters: "the increase in amount
of a color — not merely in size of canvas — visually reduces distance. As a consequence, it often
produces nearness — meaning intimacy — and respect."

## 10. Film color and volume color

**Surface color** (ch. XVII): the color we name an object by (apple red, lemon yellow, brick).

**Film color** (ch. XVII): "a thin, transparent, translucent layer between the eye and an object,
independent of the object's surface color" — distant mountains uniformly blue, the sun white at
noon and red at sunset, white eaves looking green from lawn reflection. Film color is "a physical
phenomenon", not a physiological or psychological transformation.

**Volume color** (ch. XVII): color that "exists and is perceived in 3-dimensional fluids" — only
transparent fluids show it. Coffee: lightest in the percolator stem, darker in the cup, darkest in
the glass; tea lighter in a spoon than a cup; pool water bluer with each step down. Milk, ink and
oil paint do not change with volume. Watercolor layers behave as volume color (Klee); oil, gouache,
pastel give surface color. Both effects are "tricks of nature."

## 11. The Weber-Fechner law

**Statement** (ch. XX). "The visual perception of an arithmetical progression depends upon a physical
geometric progression." If steps 1 and 2 measure 1 and 2 units, step 3 must measure 4 (not 3), then
8, 16, 32, 64 — and the eye reads these as equal steps. Named for Wilhelm Eduard Weber (1804–91) and
Gustav Theodor Fechner (1801–87). Real calculations are logarithmic and theoretically never reach
saturation.

**What goes wrong with arithmetic steps** (ch. XX). Chevreul's instruction (10 stripes, each
~1/4 inch, India ink layered 1, 2, 3 ... 10 times) does *not* produce equal steps: the rate of
increase decreases, the physically straight line "becomes psychologically a curve, ending in a
horizontal line representing saturation." Layering is additive in color but subtractive in light.
Diagram labels: "THIS PHYSICAL FACT (equal steps) REDUCES TO THIS PSYCHOLOGICAL EFFECT (shrinking
steps)"; "THIS PHYSICAL FACT (1, 2, 4, 8, 16) PRODUCES THIS PSYCHOLOGICAL EFFECT (equal steps)."

**Deflected by relativity** (ch. XX). Even a correct geometric scale is "deflected by the relativity
of color": steps not much wider than deep do not stay visually level; broader steps show the
"fluting" effect.

**Importance** (ch. XX). "Almost unknown among colorists" though recognized in physics and
psychology (sound, weight, temperature, light, color). It explains a *perceptual* phenomenon,
unrelated to light refraction (an *optical* effect).

## 12. Temperature and other polarities

**Polarity chains** (ch. XXI). Light–dark relates to light–heavy, which leads to soft–hard;
quick–slow and early–late connect with young–old, warm–cool and wet–dry; even here–there (spatial
differentiation). The 2 most comprehensive polarities: light–dark/light–heavy, and warm–cool.

**Warm–cool is relative** (ch. XXI). Western tradition: blue cool; yellow-orange-red warm. But "any
temperature can be read higher or lower in comparison with other temperatures", so "there are also
warm blues and cool reds possible within their own hues." Mixed with neutrals (white, black, grey)
and the temperature-neutrals green and violet, interpretations "may easily become disparate" and
"end in personal beliefs." The Munich school's warm–cool spatial doctrine (light cold, shadows warm
outdoors; factions over here/there, above/below, in/out; Boucher receding with vermilion vs. Rubens
advancing with white) ended "in fruitless controversies." Newer "warm near, cool far" (wave length)
claims: "optical and perceptual registration are not necessarily parallel."

## 13. Color systems and theories

**Role** (ch. XXIV). Systems are "systematic groupings of the colors of the visible sun spectrum,
presented in 2-dimensional or 3-dimensional order." Presented *at the end* of the course; "the more
a creative use of color developed, the less desirable became a merely trustful and obedient
application." They have practical value for industrial uses and limitations for painting.

**Systems named** (ch. XXIV, Preface). Goethe Triangle (9 basic colors; "probably the only one
demonstrating the relatedness of primary to secondary plus tertiary colors — in 2 dimensions";
subdivisions labelled lucid, serious, mighty, serene, melancholic; primaries / secondaries /
tertiaries; complementaries with their mixtures dominated by their primaries); Schopenhauer's
quantity wheel; Munsell Color Tree; Ostwald; Faber Birren (offspring of Ostwald); the
spectrophotometer for automatic analysis. Prepared "tuned" paper sets claiming to be failure-proof
are "the least desirable" (ch. III).

**Three sets of primaries** (ch. XXIV). Colorist (painters, designers): yellow–red–blue. Physicist:
3 other primaries, not including yellow. Psychologist: 4 primaries (adding green) plus 2 neutrals,
white and black.

## 14. Form vs. color, free studies, the masters

**Form is visually the loudest** (ch. XVIII). First free studies show "dominance of form or shape ...
over color" because outlines "as visually the loudest ... are noticed first" and color becomes
"accompaniment of shape." Paper colors are flat areas "reaching precisely from edge to edge", and
edges "as uninterrupted endless contours, again advocate shape first." Remedies: torn papers
(looser edges); later, means of softening or hardening boundaries — "smaller or greater contrasts
of hue or of light; elemental or complex shapes; curved, straight, broken, or dotted lines."
"Always keep in mind the color before the shape."

**Color relatedness** (ch. XVIII). The measure for evaluating free studies: "color juxtaposition in
which color exists for color's sake, and therefore appears autonomous, and not merely as
accompaniment to form." Whether something "has color" is as hard to define as "what is musical."

**Stripes: the shapeless format** (ch. XVIII). Narrow rectangles of equal length, varying only in
width, touching in full length, are read "almost shapeless." Vertical stripes are more practicable:
"in a left-right and therefore sideward contact, color interaction usually is more easily
comprehensible than within an up-down connection." The "equal rights" stripe study: 4 colors, none
dominating; "with fewer means toward more action."

**Themes in pairs of contrasts** read more precisely (ch. XVIII): gay–sad, young–old, major–minor;
bright–dull, early–late, active–passive.

**The masters** (ch. XIX). "To honor the masters creatively is to compete with their attitude rather
than with their results ... to create, not to revive." Re-performing a painting in paper reveals its
"color instrumentation" — climate, temperature, aroma, sound — not whether ultramarine or cobalt was
used. "Creative action ahead of retrospective reaction."

## 15. Media and reproduction

**Why paper, not paint** (ch. III). Paper gives innumerable ready colors; avoids mixing failures
(keeps "a continued active interest"); allows "repeated use of precisely the same color without the
slightest change"; needs only rubber cement and a single-edged razor blade; and excludes "texture"
(brush marks, wet/dry changes, hard/soft boundaries) "which too often only hides poor color
conception." Above all it forces constant comparison from a large collection displayed in front of
you — "a training which no palette can provide." *(extractor note: digital swatches share these
properties; the discipline of comparing many candidates side by side is the transferable part.)*

**Reproduction distorts instrumentation** (ch. XIX, V). 3-/4-color halftones render all colors as
optical mixtures of 3–4 transparent inks over white paper — "physically already of an absolutely
different instrumentation", with "false and misleading smoothness, even slickness or sweetness, and
the dramatized brightness of so-called high-key color reproduction." Paper transfers "look painted;
they do not look printed."

**Variants vs. variety** (ch. XXV terms). "Variety" has become "a pretentious recommendation for
designs of questionable merit", an excuse for "hurried changes" and "accidental and meaningless
whim"; "for variety's sake" is "a warning signal." Prefer *variant*: "a more thorough re-doing of a
whole or of a part within a given scheme", resulting from thorough study and comparison, aiming at
a new presentation, in the belief "that there is no final solution in form."
