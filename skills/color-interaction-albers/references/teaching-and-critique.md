# Teaching, learning and critique — Albers' method, transferred to design review

Albers calls his course "a studio course, or, if you prefer, a laboratory or workshop course"
(ch. XXV). Its procedures are a complete critique practice. Each rule below is his; the "in design
critique" lines say how to use it when reviewing palettes, screens or visual work.

## Contents
1. Order of learning
2. Presenting a problem
3. Trial and error, comparison, judgment

## 1. Order of learning

- **Practice before theory.** "Practice is not preceded but followed by theory" (ch. XXV); theory
  "is the conclusion of practice" (Intro). Color systems come last, when eyes are "better prepared
  and more receptive" (ch. XXIV). *In critique:* do not open with the color wheel or contrast
  ratios; open with what is seen, then explain.
- **A logical sequence in which each problem prepares the next** (ch. XXV): relativity → lighter/
  darker → reversed grounds → subtraction → after-image → mixture → transparence/space → intervals →
  intersection → quantity → film/volume → free studies → masters → Weber-Fechner → temperature →
  vibrating → vanishing → systems.
- **The aim is to see, not to know.** "What counts here — first and last — is not so-called
  knowledge of so-called facts, but vision — seeing" (Intro). "Knowledge and its application is not
  our aim: instead, it is flexible imagination, discovery, invention — taste" (ch. XVI).
- **Step-by-step learning** promotes "recognition of insight coming from experience, and evaluation
  resulting from comparison" and therefore recognition of growth of ability — "the strongest
  incentive for intensified action, for continued investigation (search instead of re-search)"
  (ch. XXV).
- **From Beschäftigungstrieb to Gestaltungstrieb** (ch. XXV): after an initial laissez-faire, shift
  early "from aimless play to directed study and work." Children build in 3-D before they draw;
  Gestalt psychology says 3-dimensionality is perceived earlier than 2-dimensionality.

## 2. Presenting a problem

- Show a sample and point to its specific effect; ask for equal effects "with similar and other
  colors — without first being told how to do it" (ch. XXV). The class "is not given reasons or
  favorable conditions" (ch. IV).
- Showing too many samples can be a handicap; for free work, stimulate with thematic pairs of
  contrasts instead (ch. XVIII).
- One distinct effect per study. The plates "aim at 1 distinct effect only, which, at the time, was
  given as an exercise, obligatory for all members of the class" (ch. XXV).
- *In critique:* state the single effect the design must achieve ("the CTA must read as the one
  thing in front") before anyone judges whether it does.

## 3. Trial and error, comparison, judgment

- "The aim of such study is to develop — through experience — by trial and error — an eye for
  color" (Intro).
- "Continuing comparison — observation — 'thinking in situations' — is promoted, making the class
  aware that discovery and invention are the criteria of creativeness" (ch. IV). "Thinking in
  situations" is "a new educational concept unfortunately little known and less cultivated" (ch.
  XXV).
- "Evaluation through comparison, which in the end means judgment" (ch. XVIII). Comparison runs
  "from student to student" (ch. XVIII, Preface) and "forth and back" (ch. XI, XXV).
- Change the conditions, not only the answer: when the needed middle color is missing, move the
  grounds (ch. VI, XI). *In critique:* if a color cannot be fixed, change its neighbors, its
  quantity or its placement.
- Several solutions per problem from every student (ch. XXV). *In critique:* ask for variants, not
  a single proposal.
- Exercises are never finished: "as basic rules of any language must be practiced continuously ...
  so exercises toward distinct color effects never are done or over" (ch. XXV).

## 4. Running the exhibition / review

Albers' sequence (ch. IV, IX, XXV):
1. All trial results are exhibited together at the start of the next class (on the floor, people
   standing around them) — the "admission tickets."
2. Sort into "groups of more and less promise": wrong, "on the way", right.
3. Every member makes a personal selection and compares those preferences with their own
   contributions.
4. Then the group (teacher or students) selects the best examples of **"psychological
   engineering"** — "a convincing presentation ... because it eliminates misleading reading of the
   study's purpose and its desired effect." A successful study "cannot be misread or
   misunderstood" (ch. IV).
5. The class states merits and shortcomings and suggests corrections (ch. IX).
6. Lead "by pointing at (or indicating only by comparison forth and back) new ways of promising
   investigation" (ch. XXV).
7. Use the "step ahead": present the next problem as something that already appeared, by accident,
   among the class's studies — "a most contagious stimulus" (ch. XXV).

Evaluation criteria Albers states:
- For systematic studies: is the intended effect demonstrated unmistakably? Precision and clean
  execution. Pleasantness is irrelevant (ch. IV, XIV).
- For free studies: **color relatedness** — color existing "for color's sake", autonomous, not an
  accompaniment to shape (ch. XVIII).
- A study must not "decorate, illustrate, represent anything, or express something — or one's self"
  (ch. IV).

## 5. The teacher's stance

- "In the end, teaching is a matter not of method but of heart. ... His enthusiastic concern with
  the student's growth counts more than how much he knows" (ch. XXV).
- "The teacher actually is right and always will gain confidence when he admits that he does not
  know, that he cannot decide, and, as it often is with color, that he is unable to make a choice
  or to give advice" (ch. XXV).
- "Good teaching is more a giving of right questions than a giving of right answers" (ch. XXV).
- "In all teaching the personal example is the strongest incentive" — the teacher does the hardest
  exercise (equal light intensity) when the class cannot (ch. XXIII).
- "All thorough study is basic, and all education is self-education"; the course is "a mutual give
  and take" (ch. XXV). "My students in color have taught me more color than have books about
  color"; the normally gifted majority taught "what not to do" (ch. XXVI).
- Wrong answers provoke doubt against the teacher rather than against one's own judgment; expect
  this and let the test, not authority, decide (ch. V).
- "Not voting can have a positive meaning" — abstaining in doubt is legitimate (ch. V).

## 6. Free studies, self-expression and taste

- Albers states "our disbelief in self-expression, either as a way of study or as its aim, in
  schools" (ch. XXV); systematic studies "permit hardly any self-expression" (ch. XVIII). Free
  studies answer the need for independent work but are judged by color relatedness.
- Preferences and dislikes "usually result from prejudices, from lack of experience and insight";
  working with disliked colors, or with 3–4 colors chosen by someone else, is the cure (ch. V,
  XVIII). "To work with the preference of others, to subordinate to someone else's palette or
  instrumentation, should be not only permitted but encouraged" (ch. XVIII).
- Restricted means: "it will again prove more profitable to aim with fewer means toward more
  action" (ch. XVIII).
- Honor precedent by competing "with their attitude rather than with their results" — "to create,
  not to revive" (ch. XIX). *In critique:* reference designs are instruments to re-perform, not
  templates to copy.
- "Variants" not "variety": a variant is a thorough re-doing within a scheme after comparison;
  "for variety's sake" is a warning signal (ch. XXV).

## 7. Vocabulary discipline

- Say "light intensity" (lightness) and "color intensity" (brightness); avoid "value" except in
  Munsell terms (ch. XXV).
- Distinguish "factual facts" (measured) from "actual facts" (perceived) (ch. XXV).
- Name the "influencing" and the "influenced" color (ch. IV); name the "boundary" quality (soft/
  hard, vibrating, vanishing); name "quantity" as amount and recurrence; name "placement" (ch. XVI).
- Articulate associations verbally — "the purpose is to stimulate again a reading of the meaning
  of form, to invite verbal formulation of reaction" (ch. XVIII) — while remembering that verbal
  reactions "differ vastly from person to person."
