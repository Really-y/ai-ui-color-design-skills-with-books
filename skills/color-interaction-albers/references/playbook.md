# Playbook — the course as reproducible exercises, plus task procedures

Part A reproduces each study of Albers' course as an exercise (goal, materials, steps, success
criteria, perceptual lesson). The sequence is the book's: "each problem prepares for following
ones" (ch. XXV). Part B turns the same material into procedures for everyday color tasks.

# A. The course, exercise by exercise

## A0. Setting up: materials and class procedure (ch. III, XXV)

**Materials.** Color paper, not paint. Build a large collection cheaply: waste strips from printers
and bookbinders; samples of packing, wrapping, bag, cover and decoration papers; cutouts from
magazines, advertisements, posters, wallpapers, paint samples, catalogues. Pool and exchange within
the group. Avoid relying on prepared system sets (Munsell, Ostwald) and especially "tuned" sets
claiming to be failure-proof. Tools: heavy rubber cement, a single-edged razor blade (not
scissors). For the finest work (A21): the thinnest razor blade, thin paper, a glass cutting ground.
*(extractor note: digitally, the equivalent is a large swatch library displayed side by side, never
a fixed "harmony" generator.)*

**Why paper.** Repetition of precisely the same color; no mixing failures; no texture hiding poor
color conception; and the obligation to pick "just the right color" from a displayed collection,
constantly comparing neighbors and contrasts.

**Standing procedure for every problem** (ch. IV, IX, XXV):
1. Show a sample study and point to its specific effect. Do *not* explain how it was done or which
   conditions favor it. The class starts "on a trial-and-error basis."
2. Students produce "equal effects with similar and other colors", mostly as homework. Several
   solutions per problem are expected from every student.
3. Results are exhibited at the start of the next class (on the floor, students standing around)
   as "admission tickets."
4. First trials are collected and sorted "into groups of more and less promise" — wrong, "on the
   way", right. Lead by pointing and by "comparison forth and back", not by lecturing.
5. Every member selects favorites and compares those preferences with their own contributions.
   Then the best examples of "psychological engineering" are chosen: presentations so convincing
   that they "cannot be misread or misunderstood."
6. Exploit the "step ahead": later problems often appear by accident among current studies; present
   the next problem as emanating from the class.
7. Precision and clean execution are required for all finished studies; "whether or not we arrive
   at a pleasant or harmonious color combination is unimportant."
8. Exercises are never "done or over": revisit each with new cases, again and again.

## A1. One color looks like two (ch. IV; Plates IV-3)

- **Goal.** Make one and the same color appear "different — and, if possible, incredibly different."
- **Materials.** 2 small rectangles cut from the *same* paper; 2 large grounds of very different
  color but identical size and shape.
- **Steps.** Place one small rectangle on each ground; keep the small pieces deliberately small on
  large grounds. Try many ground pairs; notice which grounds exert influence and which colors
  resist or accept it. Sort trials into more/less promising groups and look for the two directions
  of change: light (lighter/darker) and hue.
- **How to look.** Do not shuttle the eyes between the two centers; stare at the midpoint of the
  boundary between the two grounds to see both simultaneously. To verify the samples are identical,
  make peephole tubes with your fingers and isolate them.
- **Success.** The two identical pieces read as clearly different colors; a viewer cannot "misread"
  the demonstration. Only the grounds differ factually.
- **Failure modes.** Small pieces on small grounds; interlocked tile layouts (influence from four
  directions; bad area ratio between influencing and influenced color).
- **Lesson.** Change is a result of influence; the ground pushes the figure in light and in hue at
  once; "color is the most relative medium in art."

## A2. Lighter or darker? — the self-test (ch. V)

- **Goal.** Discover whether you can tell light intensity apart across hues.
- **Materials.** Several pairs of colors of different hue, none of equal light intensity, chosen
  to be hard.
- **Steps.** For each pair record which is darker ("visually the heavier one, or the one containing
  more black, or less white"). Abstain when in doubt. Score.
- **Expected result.** Albers' classes, year after year: 60% wrong, 40% right (undecided excluded),
  including advanced painters. Photographs do not give the answer either.
- **Follow-up.** Collect pairs where you cannot decide; paste them side by side; observe "again
  and again until their light-dark relationship is clearly recognized." These become the
  "near-equals" used in A21.
- **Lesson.** Painters and designers are usually wrong to assume they belong to the minority that
  can read lightness through hue. Abstaining ("not voting") has positive meaning.

## A3. The after-image test for light intensity (ch. V, XXIII)

- **Goal.** Decide which of two near-equal colors is darker when direct looking fails.
- **Steps.** Lay sheet B partly over sheet A. Fix your eyes on the covering corner of B "longer than
  the eye wants to." Quickly remove B. If the area that was covered now looks *lighter* than the
  rest of A, then B is the darker — and vice versa. Repeat with the papers reversed.
- **Caveat.** "Frequently only 1 of the 2 reversed comparisons reveals the true relationship."
- **Lesson.** The after-image can be turned into a measuring instrument.

## A4. Gradation studies — grey scales (ch. V)

- **Goal.** Build sensitivity to lighter/darker through even grey steps, scales, "ladders."
- **Materials.** As many paper greys as possible, not a commercial grey set (too few, unequal
  steps). Black-and-white magazine reproductions are a rich source — but they are biased toward
  very heavy and very light greys and lack middle greys.
- **Steps.** Cut greys of various sizes; arrange them in gradation from white toward black.
  Steps must be *contiguous* — "any lines or empty spaces between the steps interfere with a direct
  comparison" and are "nonsensical." Then subdivide and mount scales to create new interactions
  between graduating and non-graduating greys.
- **Success.** "The softer the steps appear, and the more equal the steps are, the more valuable
  and convincing the study."
- **Do not** build the scale by stepping up thin layers of watercolor or India ink (see A19).

## A5. Color intensity — sorting a hue (ch. V)

- **Goal.** Confront the fact that brightness (color intensity) resists group agreement.
- **Steps.** Collect all shades and tints within one hue; choose "the most typical hue (the bluest
  blue, the greenest green, etc.)" and place it within the group. Also list your own preferences
  and aversions; deliberately work with disliked colors.
- **Lesson.** A few people may agree, a class hardly ever. Preferences shift and are prejudices.

## A6. One color shows two faces — reversed grounds (ch. VI)

- **Goal.** Make 3 colors look like 2: a single color placed on two grounds should look like
  ground B when on ground A and like ground A when on ground B — "the changed color is to echo the
  2 changing ones."
- **Steps.** Ask: "Which color will play simultaneously the roles of the 2 colors of the 2
  reciprocal grounds?" Early trials look closer to one ground than the other. When no paper is
  equally close to both, change one or both grounds rather than forcing the in-between.
- **Success.** The in-between color is "topologically in the middle" of the two grounds.
- **Difficulty ladder.** Same hue, lighter/darker (easy) → two different hues (harder) →
  complementary grounds (most interesting).

## A7. Two different colors look alike — subtraction (ch. VII)

- **Goal.** The reverse of A1: make 2 different colors look alike.
- **Warm-up.** 3 reds on white (all read "red") → on a different red ground (differences in hue
  and light become obvious) → on a ground equal to one of them (that one vanishes; only 2 "show").
  Repeat with light colors on light grounds, dark on dark.
- **Steps.** Identify the hue and light difference between the two samples. Choose grounds whose
  own hue/light subtracts exactly the quality that distinguishes each sample, or push each sample
  by contrast toward the other. The more different the grounds, the stronger the influence.
- **Success.** The two factually different samples read as one color across the two grounds.
- **Lesson.** "Any ground subtracts its own hue (and light) from colors which it carries."

## A8. Experiencing the after-image (ch. VIII; Plates VIII-2)

- **Materials.** Red and white circles ca. 3 in. in diameter with a black center dot, pasted left
  and right on black card ca. 10 × 20 in. with equal black before, between and after. Second
  board: yellow circles touching, filling a white square, beside an empty white square of the same
  size, both on black.
- **Steps.** Stare at the red center up to half a minute (expect moon-sickle shapes at the edge and
  difficulty holding the fixation; keep going). Shift suddenly to the white center → green or
  blue-green. Then stare at the yellow-circle square and shift to the empty square → yellow
  *diamonds* (the leftover shapes): contrast reversal.
- **Lesson.** Simultaneous contrast is the cause of color deception, and no eye is immune.

## A9. Illusionary mixture and transparence (ch. IX)

- **Goal.** Choose a paper that convincingly reads as the mixture of two others, and make it look
  transparent.
- **Steps.** Hold a blue and a yellow together; imagine their green; pick that paper. Test with 3
  equal rectangles: blue horizontal (1); green vertical (2) overlapping the blue at its top; yellow
  on top of the green (3) with its top edge at the blue's bottom edge. The green must read as the
  in-between. Exhibit on the floor; the class names merits, shortcomings, corrections.
- **Progression.** blue+yellow→green; red+blue→violet; black+white→grey; then unusual pairs (pink
  and ochre). Then the inverse tasks: given A and C find B; given B and C find A.
- **Rules for the transparence illusion.** Colors must be in overlapping shapes; the overlap is
  the mixture's place; make the mixture area larger than the two parent areas for a stronger
  effect.
- **Lesson.** Mixture can be seen without being made; an opaque paper can be made to look
  translucent.

## A10. Additive and subtractive mixtures (ch. X)

- **Goal.** Relate the paper illusions to real light and paint behavior.
- **Steps.** In transparence-illusion layouts, make one series where mixtures get *lighter* than
  both parents (as projected light does — additive) and one where they get *darker* (as pigment
  does — subtractive). Keep it simple: 1 thin color mixed with white (or black), "and then
  reversing the first study."
- **Lesson.** Mixtures gain light only in direct light; reflected mixtures lose light.

## A11. Varying mixtures and the middle mixture (ch. XI; Plate XI-3)

- **Goal.** From one parent pair, produce mixtures with different dominance; then find the
  equidistant middle mixture.
- **Steps.** With blue and yellow, find greens with yellow dominance and with blue dominance.
  Notice: nearer one parent = farther from the other. Then search for the middle. Use the bar
  diagram logic: if the candidate is nearer the lighter parent it is too light (look for a darker
  middle); if nearer the darker it is too dark. When the needed middle is unavailable, move the
  *outer* colors instead (raise the lower, lower the upper). Demonstrate physically with three
  hands held at different heights.
- **Measure.** Read the boundaries: a softer boundary toward one parent means the mixture is nearer
  it. A true middle has all boundaries equally soft or hard and reads frontal, "as a color by
  itself."
- **Lesson.** Boundaries measure nearness and distance, i.e. the plastic (spatial) action of color.

## A12. Color intervals and transformation (ch. XIV)

- **Goal.** Transpose a 4-color constellation to a higher or lower key keeping "parallel
  intervals" of light intensity.
- **Steps.** Combine 4 equal squares of different colors into one larger square; notice how they
  pair (vertical, horizontal, diagonal, trio vs. one). Build a second group, each color raised (or
  lowered) by the same step. If 4 equally shifted colors cannot be found, change the original set.
  To compare, cut a small rectangle from the center of each group and exchange them: the
  superimposed patches show whether the stepping corresponds; also compare each patch with the
  group beneath it.
- **Constraints.** Cannot go lower if the set contains the darkest available color; cannot go higher
  past the lightest white.
- **Success.** Equal relationships in equal placement; similar boundaries in both groups. A
  pleasant look is not the aim.
- **Bonus.** Keep the step very small and you get the transparence effect called film color.

## A13. Intersecting colors — the fluting effect (ch. XV; Plate XV-2)

- **Materials.** 3 equal sheets of one hue: light, dark, and the (elusive) middle.
- **Steps.** Light sheet at left, overlapped by the middle; dark sheet on top of the middle leaving
  a strip of middle about 1/4 in. wide. Slowly slide the dark sheet right, widening the strip,
  while staring at the middle.
- **Observation.** The strip becomes 2 colors: lighter at the edge next to the dark, darker at the
  edge next to the light — the middle "plays the role of both mixture parents ... in reversed
  placement." In a true middle both parents appear in equal amounts; otherwise the predominant one
  shows. Extend to different and opposing hues; use concave cuts to encourage the "fluting"
  reading.
- **Lesson.** Reading constituents inside a mixture yields the illusion of volume; even an
  untrained eye can see the amounts.

## A14. Quantity studies — four performances of one cast (ch. XVI)

- **Goal.** With one fixed set of 4 colors ("cast"), produce 4 arrangements ("performances") on
  the same format ("stage") so different that the set reads as 4 different casts.
- **Steps.** Vary only quantity — how much (area) and how often (recurrence) — which shifts
  dominance and placement. Aim for different climate, temperature, tempo, rhythm, mood. Ask: which
  group of colors is ready to lose its identity as a cast? Which distribution of space, time and
  weight disguises the same cast?
- **Lesson.** "Any color goes with any other color, presupposing that their quantities are
  appropriate." More amount reduces distance and produces nearness, intimacy, respect.

## A15. Free studies and themes (ch. XVIII; Plate XVIII-1)

- **When.** Homework throughout the course, alongside the systematic problems.
- **Measure.** Color relatedness — color existing "for color's sake", autonomous, not an
  accompaniment to shape.
- **Prompts.** Pairs of contrasts: gay–sad, young–old, major–minor; bolder: bright–dull,
  early–late, active–passive. Restricted palettes: only contrasting or only adjacent colors;
  preferred or disliked colors; aiming at concord or discord. Hardest and most revealing: work
  with 3–4 colors chosen by someone else.
- **Lesson.** Preferences are prejudices; submitting to another's palette builds judgment.

## A16. Stripes — restricted juxtaposition (ch. XVIII)

- **Problem 1.** Many narrow vertical stripes, equal length, varying width, touching, only 4
  colors. Aim: an order where all 4 are equally important/unimportant — none dominates. Use very
  different colors so light and color intensity compete and balance. Read the result as fabric and
  as a color "climate" (new/old, young/old, periods, peoples) and put the association into words.
- **Problem 2.** Dominance of 1 or more colors and broad areas is allowed; coordination and/or
  subordination; more colors, extension, repetition — but still equal height and vertical
  partitioning "to avoid interference of form." Think of subdivided walls. Still: "with fewer
  means toward more action."
- **Why stripes.** Equal shapeless shapes stop outline from dominating; sideward contact makes
  interaction easier to read than up-down.

## A17. Fall leaf studies (ch. XVIII)

Collect, press, dry (varnish, bleach, dye if wanted) autumn leaves; use singly, in groups, in
parts, repeated, reversed, together with color paper. They add tints, shades and modulations papers
lack. "Always keep in mind the color before the shape." Best in season; preserved leaves are duller.

## A18. Re-performing the masters (ch. XIX)

Transfer a painting into color paper to identify its "color instrumentation": a general impression
of climate, temperature, aroma, sound — not a replica, not a pigment inventory. Expect reproductions
to lie (transparent inks, slick high-key brightness); the opacity and weight of paper correct them,
so transfers "look painted." Purpose: compare attitudes and temperaments "for the sake of continued
self-criticism and self-evaluation."

## A19. The Weber-Fechner demonstration (ch. XX)

- **Materials.** Adhesive-backed acetate film of the thinnest tint available ("Zip-a-tone",
  "Artype", "Cello-tak"); or waxed sandwich paper viewed against light; or thinnest whites layered
  on black paper for the reflected-light version. Avoid watercolor (uneven coats, heavy contours).
- **Steps.** On one sheet make two rows of contiguous, equal-width steps: row 1 with 1, 2, 3, 4,
  5 ... layers (Chevreul's arithmetic order); row 2 with 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 ... layers (geometric).
- **Observation.** Row 1 looks like shrinking steps approaching saturation, with boundaries getting
  softer; row 2 looks like equal steps with equally distinct boundaries. Material imprecision causes
  occasional aberrations. Broad steps show fluting.
- **Lesson.** Visually equal steps require physically geometric increments; a swimming pool's steps
  prove it outdoors.

## A20. Vibrating boundaries (ch. XXII)

Juxtapose colors strongly contrasting in hue but close in light intensity; observe shadow/reflected
light or doubled contours at the edge; note that some viewers see it and others do not, and that it
feels aggressive. Use knowingly or avoid.

## A21. Equal light intensity — vanishing boundaries (ch. XXIII; Plate XXIII-2)

- **Goal.** Two adjacent papers of different hue whose boundary disappears "through the choice of
  color alone." Usually the last exercise; often achieved only by the teacher ("the personal
  example is the strongest incentive").
- **Conditions.** Adjacent, neighboring hues only; exact equality of light intensity.
- **Steps.** Start from the "almost-equals" collected in A2; apply the after-image test (A3)
  repeatedly; watch cumulus clouds against blue sky with the sun at your back for a natural
  reference. Mount as inlay ("intarsia"): papers set within each other, of equal thickness, cut
  simultaneously in one cut with the thinnest razor blade on glass; no glue seeping into joints;
  no differing surfaces, visible edges or paste marks.
- **Success.** The underlying design (e.g. small triangles) almost dissolves into the ground.
- **Warning.** Do not expect a print or photo to preserve it.

## A22. Color systems, at the end (ch. XXIV)

Only after the exercises: Goethe triangle (with its mood subdivisions), Schopenhauer's quantity
wheel (3:4:6:9:8:6), Munsell, Ostwald, Faber Birren, the spectrophotometer; the three primaries
sets of colorist, physicist and psychologist. Note the industrial value, the limits for painting,
and that harmonies "are not the only desirable relationship."

# B. Task procedures

## B1. Judging any color: the comparison protocol

1. Never judge from memory or from a name (ch. I). Put the candidates in front of you.
2. Judge in context: on the ground it will sit on, next to the neighbors it will have, in the
   quantity and recurrence it will have, under the light it will be seen in (ch. IV, XVI).
3. Compare under equal conditions — same size, same shape, same ground — so that only the variable
   you test differs (ch. IV).
4. Compare contiguously, not across gaps or black lines; superimpose or exchange patches when two
   groups must be compared (ch. V, XIV).
5. To see two influenced colors at once, fix on the boundary between their grounds; to see a color
   without its context, isolate it (peepholes) (Preface).
6. Expect to be wrong 60% of the time on lightness across hues; test rather than assert (ch. V).
7. Treat screenshots, prints and reproductions as a different instrumentation, not as proof (ch.
   V, XIX).

## B2. Deciding which of two colors is darker

Direct look → if undecided, after-image test both ways (A3) → if still undecided, mount them
contiguously and look again over time (A2). Record "undecided" rather than guess. Remember the
darker is the visually heavier one.

## B3. Making a color look different / the same on purpose

- To make one color look like two: large, very different grounds; small identical figures;
  influence from one side only; exploit both light and hue directions (A1).
- To make two colors look alike: diagnose their difference as light and/or hue; choose grounds
  whose own light/hue subtracts that difference, or push each by contrast toward the other (A7).
- To make one color echo two grounds: pick the topological middle; if none exists, move the
  grounds (A6).
- To make an opaque color look transparent: overlapping shapes, mixture in the overlap, mixture
  area larger than the parents, mixture neither lighter nor darker than its parents (A9, ch. XI).

## B4. Building an even scale (greys, tints, shades)

- Physically geometric steps (1, 2, 4, 8, 16) read as equal; arithmetic steps compress toward
  saturation (A19).
- Verify by eye with contiguous swatches; the boundaries should look equally distinct along the
  scale; softening boundaries mean the steps are shrinking (ch. XX).
- Keep swatches wider than they are deep; broad steps may flute (ch. XX).
- Expect any scale to be "deflected by the relativity of color" once placed in a design.

## B5. Reading and controlling boundaries (space, separation, dissolution)

| Want | Do |
|---|---|
| Two areas to connect, read as near / same plane | Soften the boundary: reduce light and/or hue contrast; approach the middle mixture (ch. XI) |
| Two areas to separate, one in front | Harden the boundary: increase contrast; emphasize borders sparingly and only where separation is needed (Cézanne, ch. XI) |
| An area to sit flat and frontal | Make all its boundaries equally soft or hard (ch. XI) |
| A boundary to disappear | Adjacent hues at exact equal light intensity, flawless joint (ch. XXIII) |
| A boundary to vibrate (rarely wanted) | Contrasting hues at near-equal light intensity (ch. XXII) |
| Color to dominate over shape | Soften contours (torn edges, broken/dotted lines, smaller contrasts); use shapeless formats such as stripes (ch. XVIII) |

## B6. Deciding quantities and placement for a set of colors

1. Treat the set as a cast and the layout as a performance (ch. XVI).
2. Ask "how much" (area) and "how often" (recurrence) for each color; both set predominance and
   weight in space and time.
3. Do not give all members the same quantity, shape, count and lightness — that is the "theoretical
   and least practicable" harmony-chart presentation.
4. Try at least 4 different distributions of the same set and compare them side by side.
5. Remember: a larger amount of a color reduces its distance and creates nearness.
6. Account for changing light, several simultaneous lights, reflections, reading direction and
   sequence, materials, and neighboring objects — the recipe still needs tasting.
7. Dissonance is permitted; equilibrium of tensions (asymmetry) is as valid as harmony (symmetry).

## B7. Transposing a palette to another key

Follow A12: keep the light-intensity intervals between members parallel; accept that the exact
original members may have to change; verify by exchanging central patches, not by looking across a
gap; keep steps small if you want the film-color softness.

## B8. Running a critique session Albers-style

See `references/teaching-and-critique.md` for the full method. Minimum: exhibit all solutions
together; sort by promise; select by comparison, not by rule; name the single effect each study
aims at and judge only whether it is achieved unmistakably; ask questions rather than give
answers; expect several variants from everyone; admit when you cannot decide.
