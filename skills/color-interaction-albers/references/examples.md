# Examples — plates, demonstrations and cases, with the lesson each teaches

The 1971 text edition carries only 10 of the original 150 studies. Plate descriptions below combine
Albers' own commentary (Preface) with a description of the reproduced page where the text is silent
(marked "seen:"). Chapter numbers in parentheses.

## 1. The ten plates

**Front cover — Plate XVII-1 (transparence illusion; ch. IX, XI, XVII).** A square divided into 4:
two different reds placed diagonally, a deep green and a pale yellow. A "transparent" square sits at
the center casting slight shadings on all four, and at its right a long triangle is folded toward
the center, so a doubled transparence produces doubled shading on the red and yellow. Fact: every
paper is opaque; the "transparent" square is 6 additional, precisely related opaque shades (2 at
left for green and red; at right 2 for the other red and 2 for the yellow). Lesson: a student who
understands interaction can make us see "less (no transparence) or more (6 opaque shades)" —
"we do not see what we see." Albers calls it "a most typical Interaction study."

**Plate IV-3, upper (one color looks like two; ch. IV).** Seen: two grids of small squares cut from
one and the same green paper, one grid on a grey-blue ground, one on a yellow ground. On the grey
the green reads cool and greyed; on the yellow it reads warmer and more yellow-green. Albers: the
apparent difference "is more obvious" here than in the lower study.

**Plate IV-3, lower (one color looks like two; ch. IV).** Seen: one small dark brownish rectangle
on a large yellow ground, the same paper on a large blue ground. Albers: deliberately "small amounts
on large grounds"; to make the change noticeable "do not compare their centers by moving your eyes
back and forth. Instead, try to see the centers simultaneously by staring at a midpoint of the
boundary between the blue and yellow grounds." Isolate them with finger peepholes and they are
alike again. "If someone claims that he can see that the 2 central colors are the same ... he is
fooling only himself."

**Plate VIII-2 (after-image; ch. VIII), double page.** Seen: left page, 9 yellow circles touching
each other and filling a white square, a black fixation dot at the center, all on black; right page,
an empty white square with a center dot on black. Stare at the left for half a minute, shift to the
right: not blue (the complement) but yellow diamond shapes — the leftover spaces between the circles
— appear. Lesson: contrast reversal, a double and therefore reversed after-image. The red/white
circle demonstration precedes it (see Demonstrations).

**Plate XI-3 (transparence and space-illusion; ch. XI).** Seen: on white, 3 horizontal yellow
stripes cross 3 vertical stripes — green, violet, red — all equal in size and equidistant; the 9
crossing squares are painted in colors that read as mixtures of the crossing pair. Albers: in the top
row the horizontal boundaries of the crossing squares are the hardest/"loudest", so the top yellow
reads in front of the verticals; in the bottom row the vertical edges dominate, so the bottom yellow
reads behind; the middle row can be read either way — "an optical penetration", i.e. a middle
mixture. Cost: 12 precisely chosen paper colors. Lesson: boundary hardness places colors in space.

**Plate XV-2 (intersecting colors — fluting; ch. XV).** Seen: a diamond of nested red squares: a
central dark red, three closely related carmine reds around it, an outer light pink; the outer edges
of the red rings are cut in concave scallops. Albers: stare into the pink and each of the 3 reds
appears darker toward its outer edge and lighter toward its inner edge — each half "refers" to the
opposite neighbor. This is "color interpenetration"; the concave curves encourage the "fluting"
reading, as in the grooves of Doric columns. Lesson: a middle mixture discloses its parents in
reversed placement and produces an illusion of volume.

**Plate XVIII-1 (free study; ch. XVIII).** Seen: a grey ground carrying a large pale-yellow block
at top, a thin yellow strip, a black bar that overlaps the yellow with a grey margin around it, and a
large black rectangle below. Albers regrets a single example cannot show what free studies prove in
a class: that work from a shared set of 3–4 papers yields "an incredible variety of independent
work" and no "class", "school" or "teacher" style — unlike classes exposed only to self-expression
"without the training that comes from constant comparing from student to student."

**Plate XXIII-2 (vanishing boundaries; ch. XXIII), double page.** Seen: on one page, a design of
overlapping violet-grey triangles on white (clearly visible); facing it, the same triangle design
in grey-violet on a grey-green ground where the triangles nearly dissolve. Albers: the screen print
in the original edition proved that "precise, equal light-intensity of 2 colors (incorrectly called
'equal value') makes invisible the boundary between them"; this halftone reproduction serves only
"as a warning not to apply a photomechanical process to such color delicacies." Invitation: alter
the grey-violet and grey-green toward equal light intensity "so that the small triangles ... almost
dissolve into the ground. It is a most difficult task, but worth trying."

**Back cover — the Goethe Triangle (ch. XXIV).** Seen: an equilateral triangle, red at the apex,
yellow lower-left, blue lower-right; orange, violet and green between them; muted tertiary mixtures
in the inner triangles. Albers: 9 basic colors, "probably the only one demonstrating the relatedness
of primary to secondary plus tertiary colors — in 2 dimensions." The diagram on p. 67 subdivides it
into mood groups (lucid, serious, mighty, serene, melancholic), primaries / secondaries / tertiaries,
and "complementaries with their mixtures which are dominated by their primaries." To appreciate the
moods, cover the parts of the color triangle that are blank in each diagram.

## 2. Demonstrations described in the text

**Three pots (ch. IV).** Warm / lukewarm / cold water. Hands first in the outer pots, then both in
the middle: left hand feels cold, right feels warm — the same lukewarm water. "A discrepancy between
physical fact and psychic effect"; optical illusions deceive the same way.

**Red circle, white circle (ch. VIII).** Red and white circles ca. 3 in. across, center dots, on
black ca. 10 × 20 in. Stare at red up to half a minute (moon-sickle shapes appear; keep fixating),
shift to white: everyone sees green or blue-green, the complement of red. Lesson: simultaneous
contrast is the root of all deception.

**Three reds (ch. VII).** 3 reds on white simply read "red"; on another red their hue and light
differences show; on a red equal to one of them, that one is absorbed and only 2 show. Lesson:
subtraction of color.

**The sliding middle red (ch. XV).** Light red, middle red, dark red sheets; widen a 1/4-inch strip
of the middle by sliding the dark sheet: the strip turns into two colors, light next to dark, dark
next to light. Lesson: a middle mixture plays both parents in reversed placement.

**Three hands (ch. XI).** Two hands held one above the other are the parents; a third hand between
them is the middle mixture. Move the middle hand, or move the outer hands singly or together: every
"correct placement" problem can be solved from either end.

**The exchanged center (ch. XIV).** Cut a small central rectangle out of the 4-square group and out
of its transposed twin and swap them. The superimposed patches reveal at once whether the stepping
corresponds. Lesson: compare by superimposition, not across a gap.

**Chevreul's ten stripes vs. the geometric row (ch. XX).** Layers 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 ... look like
shrinking steps approaching saturation; layers 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 ... look like equal steps. Diagram
captions: "This physical fact reduces to this psychological effect" / "This physical fact produces
this psychological effect." Lesson: Weber-Fechner.

**The lighter/darker self-test (ch. V).** Hard pairs of different hues, none equal; 60% wrong,
40% right, year after year, painters included. Two expected class questions — "are there equal
light values within these couples?" and "will a photograph reveal their true relationship?" — both
answered No.

## 3. Cases from painting, nature and everyday life

**Ensor, "Masks Confronting Death" (1888), two reproductions (ch. V).** The expensive fine-screen
catalogue plate on coated paper vs. a coarse newspaper halftone on cheap paper. The newspaper was
"much more correct in its whole tonality" and showed one more face — a small light frontal face near
the left edge — which the "high-key" catalogue version "blotted out entirely." Lesson: higher key
loses middle greys; reproductions are not evidence.

**Mondrian color slides (ch. V).** Color film exaggerates blue and red and turns whites greenish,
making slides of Mondrian "unbearable." Lesson: color reproduction flatters at the cost of nuance.

**Cézanne (ch. XI).** Color areas with distinct and indistinct endings, with and without boundaries,
as the means of plastic organization; emphasized borders used sparingly, only where spatial
separation was needed, so that evenly painted areas do not look flat.

**Impressionists and Pointillists (ch. XII).** Yellow and blue dots instead of mixed green; the
small dot size shows optical mixture depends on size and distance; the same principle gave halftone
and 3/4-color printing.

**Bezold's rugs (ch. XIII).** The whole color combination of a design changed by adding or changing
one color only.

**Boucher vs. Rubens (ch. XXI).** Boucher recedes and deepens with vermilion red; Rubens advances
and heightens with white — opposite spatial uses of warm color, illustrating why warm–cool
doctrine ended in "fruitless controversies."

**Klee's watercolors (ch. XVII, XX).** Layers increase darkness, weight and intensity (volume
color) with a decreasing rate of increase.

**Coffee, tea, pool (ch. XVII, XX).** Same coffee: lightest in the percolator stem, darker in the
cup, darkest in the glass; tea lighter in a spoon than a cup; a blue-walled pool gets bluer at each
step down, and the step edges soften downward (Weber-Fechner), while milk, ink and oil paint stay
the same in any volume.

**Mountains, sunset, green eaves (ch. XVII).** Distant mountains uniformly blue, the sun white at
noon and red at sunset, white eaves looking green above a lawn: film color, a physical layer between
eye and object.

**Cumulus clouds (ch. XXIII).** Sunlit tops white against deep blue; the shaded grey undersides are
of the same light intensity as the adjacent blue, so "we do not see where clouds end and where sky
begins" — nature's vanishing boundary (sun at your back).

**Coca-Cola red (ch. I).** The one red everyone has seen innumerable times; still, 50 listeners
imagine 50 reds and pick different samples. Lesson: visual memory and color names fail.

**"Good morning to you" (ch. XIV).** Four notes recognized in any key or instrument because the
intervals stay the same — the model for color transformation.

**Reading type (ch. II).** Words are read as "word pictures"; all-capitals are hardest; context
decides legibility — the model for reading color in context.

**Interiors and do-it-yourself schemes (ch. XVI).** "Ideal" harmony sets applied to furniture and
textiles appear "changed, lost, and reversed" under real light, materials and neighbors.

## 4. Negative examples Albers calls out

- Small pieces on small grounds; interlocked tile layouts (ch. IV) — the effect annuls itself.
- Grey scales separated by lines or gaps; scales built from arithmetic ink layers (ch. V, XX).
- Harmony charts with every member in the same quantity, shape, count and lightness (ch. XVI).
- "Tuned" color-paper sets that claim to be failure-proof (ch. III).
- Texture and brush marks that hide poor color conception (ch. III).
- Vibrating boundaries used for a "screaming effect in advertising" (ch. XXII).
- Calling colors "equal value" without having seen equality (ch. XXIII, XXV).
- "For variety's sake" as a justification (ch. XXV).
- Claiming to see colors independent of their illusionary changes (ch. VIII).
- Expecting a photograph or reproduction to prove a color relationship (ch. V, XIX, XXIII).
