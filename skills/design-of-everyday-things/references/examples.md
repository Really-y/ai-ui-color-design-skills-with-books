# Examples and cases — compressed, with the lesson each teaches

Grouped by theme. Chapter in parentheses. Use these to recognize patterns in your own product and to explain a diagnosis to others.

## 1. Doors, switches, faucets, sinks

- **The post-office glass doors (ch. 1)**: two rows of hingeless-looking glass doors; the friend pushed on the hinge side and believed himself trapped. Lesson: beauty that hides the supporting pillar destroys discoverability; a vertical plate or visible hinge is a natural signifier that costs no aesthetics.
- **Identical hardware on a push door and a pull door (ch. 1)**: the ribbed bar signifies "push" on both, so signs were added. Lesson: when a door needs a sign, the design failed; let the hardware's affordance match the action (plate = push, handle = pull).
- **Amtrak toilet door (ch. 1)**: handle signifies "pull"; the door must be rotated and slid. Lesson: sliding doors are seldom signified; a misleading perceived affordance is worse than none.
- **Hotel sink stopper (ch. 1, 4)**: no visible control; the answer was to push the stopper down into dirty water. Lesson: missing signifier plus a bad decision (dirtying clean hands) — fix the decision, not just the label.
- **Push-to-open cabinet latch (ch. 4)**: a glass door shows there is no room to open inward, so pushing seems contradictory; users pry it with fingernails and knives. Lesson: clever mechanisms need a signifier; designers refusing to "mar" the surface trade usability for looks.
- **Paris Métro doors (ch. 4)**: a passenger waited for doors that must be opened by the rider and missed his stop. Lesson: unknown conventions cause failure even in situations that look identical to familiar ones.
- **Car door handles (ch. 4)**: recessed exterior handles with horizontal slits (pull) or vertical slits (slide) tell both place and mode of action; interior handles remain poor. Lesson: form can carry the whole instruction.
- **Flaps vs landing gear (ch. 4, 5)**: identical adjacent switches led pilots to retract the wheels on the ground often enough for an NTSB report; the principles had been known for fifty years. Lesson: controls for different purposes must look and feel different (shape coding).
- **Del Mar light switches (ch. 4)**: a row of four and a column of six identical switches; "you'll get used to it" never happened. Fix: switches mounted on a floor plan of the room, each at its light's position, on a tilted plate (vertical would be ambiguous; horizontal would collect coffee cups — the tilt is an anti-affordance). Lesson: match the layout of controls to the layout of what they control; components to do this still do not exist.
- **Auditorium light controls (ch. 4)**: nobody can dim only the screen area; electricians are not trained in task analysis. Lesson: controls must come from an analysis of the activity; coordination failures across trades produce confusing designs.
- **Activity-centered lecture console (ch. 4)**: raising the room lights to answer a question exited "lecture" mode and retracted the screen. Lesson: activity presets must allow manual adjustment without cancelling the activity.
- **Dormitory taps (ch. 4)**: a pamphlet explained that taps are pushed, not turned; many attendees needed help. Lesson: if it must be pushed, make it look pushable.
- **Faucet handles (ch. 4)**: round knobs should both rotate the same direction for volume; blade handles should both be pulled; "mirror-image" installations fail with one soapy hand. Single-lever faucets are elegant and unusable because nobody standardized which axis means temperature and which direction means more. Lesson: standardize the psychological model of the action; "if all else fails, standardize."
- **Shower with distant controls (ch. 4)**: 5 s delay between turning the faucet and the water changing causes the scald-and-freeze dance. Lesson: physics delays are not fixable, but poor mapping makes them dangerous.
- **Gesture faucets and dryers (ch. 3)**: perfect mapping, no signifier; users cannot tell "broken," "empty," "wrong gesture" or "not gesture-operated" apart. Lesson: mapping without signifiers kills discoverability.

## 2. Conceptual models gone wrong and right

- **The refrigerator (ch. 1)**: two compartments, two labeled controls; in reality one thermostat and one cooling unit with a damper, a sensor of unknown location, and 24 hours before feedback. Even knowing the real model, Norman could not set it. The manufacturer still sells it. Lesson: controls that imply a false model make correct use impossible; feedback delay compounds it; the fix is either real independence (two sensors and controls) or a controller that hides the coupling.
- **Thermostat as a valve (ch. 2)**: people set thermostats and ovens to maximum to heat faster; most are on-off switches, so this only overshoots and wastes energy. Lesson: when a device offers no evidence of its operation, people invent a plausible wrong model and act on it.
- **Nest-style thermostat (ch. 2)**: shows current temperature, target, heating/cooling color, and a prediction ("will be 72° in 20 minutes"), plus a phone app with energy history. Lesson: an explicit model and prediction turn an opaque device into a collaborator.
- **The five-button watch (ch. 1)**: buttons do different things per mode, with short, long and simultaneous presses; only the manual explains it. Lesson: no mapping, no constraints, hidden modes — the paradox of technology in miniature.
- **Scissors (ch. 1)**: holes are affordance, signifier and constraint at once; wrong fingers still work. Lesson: good everyday design makes the model obvious and tolerates imprecision.
- **Files and folders, the cloud (ch. 1)**: a helpful simplified model that breaks when the connection drops and the user cannot save. Lesson: simplified models are valuable only while their assumptions hold; show what changed when they break.
- **Steering: wheel, tiller, tracks, countersteering (ch. 1, 3)**: all mappings work because each has a compelling story; a motorcycle rider's turn-signal rule ("the switch moves like the handlebar") is physically wrong but behaviorally right. Lesson: the usefulness of a model is in the behavior it produces, not its accuracy.

## 3. Feedback failures and fixes

- **Elevator and crosswalk buttons; bicycles at intersections (ch. 1)**: no acknowledgment, so people press repeatedly or wait wondering if they were detected. Lesson: acknowledge every request.
- **Filing cabinet (ch. 2)**: the handle and slider signified correctly, but when the drawer jammed the cabinet gave no information about whether wiggling or twisting helped. Lesson: the evaluation gulf opens exactly when things go wrong.
- **Dishwasher beeping at 3 a.m.; backseat drivers; cockpit alarm cacophony (ch. 1, 5)**: too much or uncoordinated feedback leads people to ignore or disable everything. Lesson: prioritize and coordinate.
- **Delayed button response (ch. 2)**: repeated presses intensify or toggle the effect. Lesson: respond within 0.1 s; show progress and underpredict durations.
- **Inward-opening fire exits (ch. 2)**: people push harder when nothing happens; laws now require outward doors with panic bars. Lesson: design for the real behavior under stress, using affordance plus a graceful signifier (the colored bar).
- **Return vs Enter (ch. 2)**: the wrong key destroyed minutes of work; the designer blamed the manual; staff admitted "we do that a lot" but never reported it because they blamed themselves. Lesson: self-blame hides design faults; similar keys with destructive consequences need a different design, not training.
- **Electric cars (ch. 4)**: silence at low speed endangers blind and distracted pedestrians; proposed rules require sound under 30 km/h, when stationary with the system on, and in reverse. Lesson: absence of an expected signifier is a safety problem; sound must alert and orient without annoying.

## 4. Memory, knowledge in the world, reminders

- **Pennies, new coins (ch. 3)**: people hold only enough description to tell familiar coins apart; the Susan B. Anthony dollar, the pound coin and the French 10-franc coin collided with existing coins; newcomers were not confused. Lesson: new items must differ on the dimensions people already use (size for US coins, print for US bills).
- **Epic poets, reassembly (ch. 3)**: rhyme plus meaning constrain word choice (0–4% correct with one cue, 100% with both); washers before nuts and clockwise tightening cut 3.5 million orders to a handful. Lesson: constraints replace memorization.
- **Passwords (ch. 3)**: the most common are "password", "123456", "12345678", "qwerty", "abc123"; forced quarterly changes end up on Post-its; security experts propped a secure door with a brick to reach the toilets. Lesson: the more complex the requirements, the less secure the system; design for human memory.
- **Nurses writing on hands (ch. 3)**: automatic logout after interruptions erased the information they needed, so they used skin as the world. Lesson: assume interruption; keep critical information visible.
- **Pilots and ATC instructions (ch. 3)**: write it down, key it into the equipment as heard, remember the rest as familiar phrases. Lesson: let information be captured at the moment it arrives.
- **Book against the door, keys under the book (ch. 3)**: a reminder with both signal and message, at the place and time of need. Lesson: put reminders on the thing itself.
- **Microsoft calendar input (ch. 2)**: accepts "a week from Thursday", "15.35", phone numbers with any separators. Lesson: parse what people naturally write; never demand an arbitrary format.
- **Safe combination under "Mr. and Mrs. Safe" (ch. 3)**: burglars know the "unlikely places." Lesson: shared logic makes hiding places predictable.

## 5. Constraints and forcing functions

- **Lego motorcycle (ch. 4)**: fifteen pieces assembled without instructions through physical, cultural (red brake light rear, blue police light top), semantic (rider faces forward) and logical (last piece, last slot) constraints; the 2013 version has 29 pieces and needs instructions (ch. 7). Lesson: constraints make instructions unnecessary; featuritis undoes it.
- **Cylindrical batteries (ch. 4)**: fit two ways, one harmful; the compartment markings are black on black. Alternatives: symmetric battery, orientation-free contacts (InstaLoad, unadopted even by its inventor), shaped batteries. Lesson: the legacy problem keeps bad designs alive; or solve the root need (keyless access) instead of the artifact.
- **ATM card before cash (ch. 5)**: a forcing function exploiting the fact that people rarely forget the goal. Lesson: order the sequence so the goal pulls the easily forgotten step.
- **Stairway gate at ground floor (ch. 4)**: enough restraint to notice you are leaving the ground floor, not enough to prop open. Lesson: calibrate nuisance against safety.
- **Restroom shelf that blocks the door (ch. 4)**: you cannot leave without taking your bag. Lesson: a forcing function can be purely spatial.
- **Dead man's switch bypassed with a weight (ch. 4)**: so later designs require mid-level pressure or periodic responses. Lesson: expect bypass and design against it.
- **Exit-without-saving prompt (ch. 4, 6)**: a lock-in that became Norman's preferred exit path because the desired action is offered in the message. Lesson: well-designed "error" prompts become features.
- **Car fluid filling points (ch. 5)**: segregated locations, different opening shapes and fluid colors. Lesson: combine physical constraints, separation and coding to prevent description-similarity errors.
- **Poka-yoke dots (ch. 5)**: green stickers beside keyholes show which way to turn the key. Lesson: cheap signifiers remove small, frequent errors.

## 6. Culture, conventions and changing them

- **The remote with two stacked buttons (ch. 3)**: audiences worldwide split on whether "top" means next. Lesson: metaphors differ (the person moves vs the content moves); pick one and signify it.
- **Scrolling direction (ch. 3)**: two decades of "move the window," then touch screens introduced "move the text," confusing everyone who switches. Lesson: metaphor switches cost; consistency within an ecosystem matters more than which is "right."
- **Destination-control elevators (ch. 4)**: enter the floor outside, get assigned a car, no buttons inside; faster for all, invented 1985, first installed 1990, still provoking complaints. Lesson: people object to any change in convention regardless of merit; only vastly superior changes justify the cost.
- **Metric system; decimal time; Swatch .beats (ch. 4, 6)**: superior schemes that fail against entrenched habits. Lesson: timing and legacy dominate; standardize neither too early nor too late.
- **Headlight flashing (ch. 4)**: means "I'm going first" in Mexico and "you go first" in England. Lesson: a signal is only a convention; without a shared one it is dangerous.
- **HDTV standard (ch. 6)**: thirty-five years, a political fight over details most people could not see, a 16:9 ratio chosen because it is 4:3 squared. Lesson: standards are necessary, slow, political, and often overtaken by technology.
- **Skeuomorphic sounds for electric cars (ch. 4)**: marketing wanted brand sounds, some blind people wanted engine sounds. Lesson: familiar forms ease transitions by letting existing models be modified rather than replaced.

## 7. Slips, mistakes and accidents

- **Coffee cup in the refrigerator; wristwatch unbuckled instead of the seatbelt; shirt into the toilet; "9, 10, Jack, Queen, King" (ch. 5)**: everyday slips of capture and description similarity. Lesson: slips come from similarity and automaticity, not stupidity; experts make more of them.
- **Gimli Glider (ch. 5)**: fuel computed in pounds instead of kilograms (knowledge-based mistake) and an interrupted troubleshooting (memory-lapse mistake). Lesson: unit ambiguity and interruptions belong in the design, not the blame.
- **Airbus descent modes (ch. 5)**: −3.3 entered as an angle was read as −3,300 ft/min; the fix was different digit counts for the two modes. Lesson: modes with similar inputs and different meanings must be visibly different.
- **Royal Majesty (ch. 5)**: a disconnected GPS cable silently switched navigation to dead reckoning ("dr" in tiny letters); years of reliability had ended manual checks; the ship ran aground. Lesson: automation failure must announce itself; trust erodes verification.
- **F-22 crash (ch. 5)**: "pilot failed to recover" vs "the pilot was probably unconscious." Lesson: root cause analysis must continue past the human.
- **Utility electrocutions (ch. 5)**: committees stopped at worker fault; "no changes, still injuring people." Lesson: a "we don't make errors" culture blocks redesign.
- **Kiss nightclub fire (ch. 5)**: outdoor flares indoors; restrooms mistaken for exits; guards blocking exits to stop drink-dodgers. Lesson: rules written without the emergency case kill; alarmed exit doors satisfy both goals.
- **Oven at maximum plus wandering off (ch. 5)**: a mistake (false model) and a memory-lapse slip combine into a fire. Lesson: errors stack.
- **Anti-lock brakes (ch. 5)**: the pulsing feels like malfunction, so drivers lift their foot. Lesson: unfamiliar feedback is misread; teach or redesign the signal.
- **Tenerife and Air Florida (ch. 5)**: delays, weather, authority gradients and explaining away ("something's not right," four times). Lesson: social pressure is part of the system; reward stopping.
- **Scuba weights (ch. 5)**: the instructor publicly praised and reimbursed anyone who dropped weights. Lesson: remove the social penalty for the safe action.
- **Wrong turn toward Las Vegas (ch. 5)**: billboards were explained away for two hours. Lesson: anomalies get rationalized; most should be, which is why true ones are missed.
- **Deleting "my most important file" (ch. 5)**: confirmations are answered automatically because people focus on the action, not the object. Lesson: show the object prominently, offer undo, keep a trash.
- **Closing the wrong window (ch. 5)**: a slip plus two mistakes (not reading an expected dialog). Lesson: warnings are weak against mistakes; make the object visible and the action reversible (secretly keep the unsaved contents).
- **Million-won transfer; 1000x radiation dose (ch. 5)**: systems meekly comply with absurd values. Lesson: sensibility checks against normal ranges.
- **Medication barcode scanning (ch. 5)**: scan prescription and drug, drug and wristband; flag repeats. Lesson: perceptible checks at each step with little added workload.
- **Checklists in aviation vs medicine (ch. 5)**: decades to accept; physicians say "not me." Lesson: being human is not a threat to competence.
- **Sterile cockpit (ch. 5)**: no unrelated talk at takeoff and landing; Norman's family does the same when merging onto highways. Lesson: protect critical phases from interruption.
- **Boeing 787 battery fires (ch. 5 notes)**: redundant layers kept everyone safe; the fleet was still grounded for investigation. Lesson: proactive practice before harm.

## 8. Design process and business cases

- **Students solving the assigned problem brilliantly (ch. 6)**: "How do you know you solved the correct problem?" Lesson: discover the problem before solving it.
- **Wizard of Oz airline assistant (ch. 6)**: users said "back before my first class at 9 AM"; understanding sentences was not enough, the system needed to understand activities. Lesson: cheap simulations reveal the real requirement.
- **"Next time we'll do it right" (ch. 6)**: four to five weeks of field research is refused on every project. Lesson: keep researchers in the field permanently.
- **Copier purchasing (ch. 6)**: price plus feature list; usability, training and maintenance never considered though they cost more later. Lesson: study and influence the purchaser.
- **OXO peeler (ch. 6)**: designed for an arthritic wife, sold as better for everyone, many times the price of a cheap peeler and still cheap. Lesson: inclusive design, marketed without stigma, wins the whole market.
- **Two-latch school door (ch. 6)**: deliberately hard so children cannot leave without an adult. Lesson: difficulty can be the requirement; make only that part hard.
- **Startup cooking equipment and the Zeitgeist (ch. 7)**: competitors showed similar concepts before launch; distributors were the real customers. Lesson: ideas are in the air; execution and timing decide.
- **Apple QuickTake, digital picture frame, Fingerworks, videophone (ch. 7)**: good ideas too early fail and return later under other names; multitouch took about thirty years; the videophone 140. Lesson: twenty years lab-to-product, then a decade or two to adoption.
- **QWERTY (ch. 7)**: shaped by typebar mechanics and perhaps a sales trick; Dvorak is only slightly faster; alphabetical layouts are no faster than random; chord keyboards are fast but all knowledge is in the head. Lesson: legacy plus "good enough" beats theoretical optimality; walk-up usability needs knowledge in the world.
- **Ford's scrapped cars (ch. 7)**: looking for parts that outlasted the rest to make them fail sooner. Lesson: business incentives can oppose durability; design has moral weight.
- **Freestyle chess (ch. 7)**: amateurs with three computers and a better process beat grandmasters and supercomputers. Lesson: design the human-machine process, not just the machine.
- **POET to DOET (acknowledgments)**: a cute title shelved under psychology; Norman researched his readers and changed it. Lesson: apply HCD to your own artifacts, including names.
