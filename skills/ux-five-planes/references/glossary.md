# Glossary: the author's terms (Garrett, *The Elements of User Experience*, 2nd ed.)

Alphabetical. Chapter in parentheses.

- **Action button** (ch. 6): control that takes the information the user provided via other elements
  and acts on it.
- **Architecture diagram** (ch. 5): the internal visual document of a product's structure; preferred
  over "site map", which names a user-facing navigation tool.
- **Bottom-up architecture** (ch. 5): categories derived from analyzing existing/planned content and
  functional requirements, grouped upward.
- **Brand identity** (ch. 3): a set of conceptual associations or emotional reactions users form about
  the organization; inescapable, so make it an explicit objective.
- **Card sorting** (ch. 3): users sort cards naming content items into natural groups to reveal how
  they categorize information.
- **Checkbox / radio button / text field / dropdown list / list box** (ch. 6): standard interface
  elements for independent options / one of mutually exclusive options / text entry / compact
  exclusive choice (hides options) / compact multiple choice (scrolls).
- **Conceptual model** (ch. 5): users' impression of how an interactive component behaves (thing
  consumed, place visited, object acquired; container; order form).
- **Content inventory** (ch. 4): a (large) spreadsheet listing all existing content.
- **Content management system (CMS)** (ch. 4): software automating the workflow to produce and deliver
  content; its needed functionality follows from the content requirements.
- **Content requirements** (ch. 4): scope-plane description of the content elements required (purpose,
  types, size, owner, frequency, audience).
- **Contextual inquiry** (ch. 3): the most powerful user-research toolkit, derived from anthropology;
  studies users in the context of their everyday lives; costly.
- **Contextual (inline) navigation** (ch. 6): links embedded in page content.
- **Controlled vocabulary** (ch. 5): the set of standard terms used on the product, drawn from users'
  language.
- **Conversion rate** (ch. 1): percentage of users who take the next step in a relationship (sign-up,
  purchase); a UX-sensitive ROI measure.
- **Courtesy navigation** (ch. 6): access to rarely needed but convenient items (contact, feedback,
  policies).
- **Design by default / mimicry / fiat** (ch. 8): unconscious decision modes: UX follows the technology
  or org structure / copies other products' conventions / follows personal preference.
- **Design comp (composite)** (ch. 7): visual mock-up built from chosen components; surface analog of
  the wireframe.
- **Error handling layers** (ch. 5): prevention (impossible, then difficult), correction (notice and
  fix), recovery (undo), warning (unrecoverable).
- **Facet** (ch. 5): a content attribute usable as an organizing principle.
- **Feature** (ch. 4): in the book, either a software function or a content offering.
- **Functional specifications** (ch. 4): the scope-plane document describing the feature set;
  "requirements" are its contents.
- **Global navigation** (ch. 6): access to the broad sweep of the whole site (key access points).
- **Hierarchical (tree, hub-and-spoke) structure** (ch. 5): nodes in parent/child relationships up to
  a root; the most common structure.
- **Index** (ch. 6): alphabetical list of topics with links; a remote navigation tool for large, diverse
  content.
- **Information architecture** (ch. 5): the structure-plane element on the information side: the
  arrangement of content elements to facilitate human understanding; organizational and navigational
  schemes.
- **Information design** (ch. 6): skeleton-plane element on both sides: presenting information so people
  can use and understand it (visual choices, grouping, error and instructional messages).
- **Interaction design** (ch. 5): structure-plane element on the functionality side: describing possible
  user behavior and how the system accommodates and responds to it.
- **Interface design** (ch. 6): skeleton-plane element on the functionality side: selecting and arranging
  interface elements so users can interact with functionality.
- **Local navigation** (ch. 6): access to what is nearby in the architecture (parent, siblings, children).
- **Matrix structure** (ch. 5): nodes navigable along two or more dimensions; more than three breaks as
  primary navigation.
- **Metadata** (ch. 5): "information about information": a structured description of a content piece.
- **Navigation design** (ch. 6): skeleton-plane element on the information side: the screen elements
  letting users move through the information architecture.
- **Node** (ch. 5): the basic unit of an information structure, at whatever level of detail you choose.
- **Nomenclature** (ch. 5): descriptions, labels and terminology used on the product.
- **Organic structure** (ch. 5): nodes connected case by case with no consistent pattern or sections.
- **Organizing principle** (ch. 5): the criterion deciding which nodes are grouped together.
- **Persistent navigation** (ch. 6): elements appearing throughout the site; not necessarily global.
- **Persona (user model, user profile)** (ch. 3): a fictional character representing the needs of a
  range of real users.
- **Product as functionality / product as information** (ch. 2): the two faces of every plane: tasks and
  tools vs finding and understanding information.
- **Product objectives** (ch. 3): the organization's internal objectives for the product; preferred over
  "business goals".
- **Psychographic profile** (ch. 3): users' attitudes and perceptions about the world or the subject.
- **Remote navigation tools** (ch. 6): stand-alone navigation aids (site map, index) used when the
  embedded navigation fails the user.
- **Ripple effect** (ch. 2): decisions on one plane constrain or force re-evaluation of decisions on
  other planes, in both directions.
- **Scenario** (ch. 4): a short narrative of a persona trying to fulfil a need; a source of requirements.
- **Scope creep** (ch. 4): unmanaged accumulation of requirements, "a snowball rolling downhill".
- **Sequential structure** (ch. 5): ordered nodes, as in books, articles, audio, video.
- **Site map** (ch. 6): a one-page hierarchical outline of the site for users; no more than two levels.
- **Stakeholders** (ch. 3): senior decision-makers responsible for parts of the organization affected
  by the product.
- **Strategy (vision) document** (ch. 3): records objectives and user needs with an analysis of their
  relationships; concise; shared with the whole team.
- **Success metrics** (ch. 3): indicators tracked after launch to tell whether objectives and user needs
  are met.
- **Supplementary navigation** (ch. 6): shortcuts to related content not reachable through global or
  local navigation.
- **Task analysis** (ch. 3): close examination of the precise steps users take to accomplish a task.
- **Thesaurus** (ch. 5): a controlled vocabulary that also maps unapproved terms to approved ones and
  records broader/narrower/related relationships.
- **Top-down architecture** (ch. 5): categories derived directly from product objectives and user
  needs, then subdivided.
- **Usability** (ch. 3): variously defined; its core, "users need usable products", is the most universal
  user need.
- **User acceptance testing** (ch. 8): end-of-process evaluation whose name reveals the low bar ("can
  they accept it?"); too late to fix what it finds.
- **User-centered design** (ch. 1): taking the user into account every step of the way so that every
  aspect of the experience results from a conscious decision.
- **User experience** (ch. 1): how a product works on the outside, where a person comes into contact
  with it.
- **User needs** (ch. 3): goals for the product that come from its users, outside the organization.
- **User segmentation** (ch. 3): dividing the audience into groups sharing key characteristics; keep
  only as many segments as distinct need sets.
- **User testing** (ch. 3): having users test what you have produced (product, work in progress or
  prototype).
- **Visual Vocabulary** (ch. 5): Garrett's standard shape notation for architecture diagrams (2000).
- **Wayfinding** (ch. 6): helping people understand where they are and where they can go; navigation
  design plus non-navigational information-design cues.
- **Wireframe (page schematic)** (ch. 6): bare-bones, annotated depiction of all components of a page and
  how they fit together.
